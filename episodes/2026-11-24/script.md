## Intro
Good morning Thomas, it's Tuesday the twenty-fourth of November. Here's your Daily Brief. Today: Bart De Wever tells his coalition that without a budget by New Year's Eve, the government is finished. OpenAI gives ChatGPT's chat a new model and finally brings its Dots agents to Europe. And a cold snap and a Norwegian outage push European gas and power prices higher.

## AI
We start with AI, and with OpenAI quietly reshuffling what sits behind ChatGPT.

On Monday, the ChatGPT account on X announced that a version of GPT-6.2 Sol is rolling out in the Chat tab, for people on the Plus, Pro, Business and Enterprise plans. According to the product news site TestingCatalog, which follows these releases closely, OpenAI says the model is tuned for everyday conversation. It is meant to handle complex requests better, read images more accurately, and suggest useful follow-ups. One example OpenAI gives is offering to set an alert when the price of something you asked about drops.

The interesting detail is what did not change. TestingCatalog notes that the models behind ChatGPT's Work tab and behind Codex, OpenAI's coding tool, stay as they are. So ChatGPT is no longer one model in one box. It's a product with several tabs, each with a model chosen for a different kind of job. Chat gets a model tuned for conversation. Work and coding keep the models tuned for longer, heavier tasks.

The second part of the news matters more for you in Belgium. Dots, OpenAI's always-on agents, are now launching in the European Union. Dots are personal agents that each get their own virtual computer and can carry on with a task between conversations, rather than waiting for you to type. When OpenAI launched them in the autumn, the EU was left out of the first rollout. TestingCatalog adds that users have spotted, but OpenAI has not confirmed, a travel mode for Dots and a redesigned ChatGPT home screen with a separate tab for your Dot.

So why does this matter for a product manager? Two lessons. First, routing by job. Instead of one model for everything, OpenAI matches the model to the task the user is doing in that part of the product. That's a design choice you can copy: decide which jobs need speed and warmth, and which need depth, and don't pay for depth everywhere. Second, Europe often gets features later. If you build on these platforms, plan your roadmap for a gap between the American launch and the European one.

Staying with the big platforms, but a less flattering story. According to Axios, Meta says its greenhouse gas emissions rose by forty-five per cent last year, driven by the data centres it is building for AI. Its electricity use went up by thirty-five per cent. In its new sustainability report, Meta says it is re-evaluating its goal of reaching net zero by twenty thirty, a target it set in twenty twenty. It has not said what it might change or when. Two Meta executives wrote that reality had been "far more complex" than expected. Axios compares that with Google, whose emissions grew eighteen per cent, Amazon at sixteen and Microsoft at twenty-five. Meta says it has contracted more than thirty gigawatts of clean energy, but less than half of that is producing power yet. We'll come back to AI and the power grid in the energy section.

Next, a warning for anyone who sends data to AI models through a middleman. The newsletter The Neuron, summarising reporting by Ars Technica and The Hacker News, describes an investigation into almost two million prompts that could be traced back to their senders. Researchers found secrets, such as passwords and access keys, that had been stolen or mishandled along the way. They say at least three hosting providers were compromised. OpenRouter, a popular service that passes your requests on to many different AI providers, has removed three providers. It also withdrew the zero data retention label, its promise that your data isn't stored, from a hundred and twenty-five endpoints. The lesson is simple: every extra company between your app and the model is one more place where your users' data can leak.

And a follow-up on Nvidia, after Jensen Huang's resignation that we covered recently. According to the newsletter Ten Things, the American Treasury Secretary, Scott Bessent, has demanded answers from Nvidia about chip sales to China. Nvidia's chairman has said the board will not take Huang back, even though President Trump has backed a possible return. Beijing denies the spying allegations behind the case.

## Politics
Over to politics, and to Belgium first, where the Prime Minister has now set a date for his own government's end.

On Monday morning, the core cabinet met again, with Vooruit at the table. It didn't work. In a statement after his visit to the palace, Bart De Wever said, as VRT News reports it, that it had become clear an agreement on the budget framework is impossible within the core cabinet. He added that one coalition partner does not accept the balance of his proposal. VRT and The Brussels Times both read that as a reference to Vooruit. According to The Brussels Times, the MR's deputy prime minister, David Clarinval, said Vooruit had refused De Wever's latest proposal in the morning round.

Then came the ultimatum. According to Reuters, De Wever set the thirty-first of December as the deadline. His statement says that if there is no budget agreement when the provisional twelfths take effect, the government will in practice no longer be able to govern, and its existence will come to an end. Provisional twelfths, as we explained yesterday, are the emergency rule that lets the state keep spending month by month without a full budget. They start in January.

King Philippe, for his part, again sent De Wever back to work. The palace said the King regrets the lack of meaningful progress, but encouraged the Prime Minister to keep trying to reach an agreement as soon as possible. According to the news agency IANS, it was De Wever's fourth audience with the King this month. He first offered his resignation on the second of November, and the King refused it.

What's next? The leaders of the five governing parties, not just the deputy prime ministers, are due to meet De Wever at four o'clock this afternoon. The disagreement is unchanged. The coalition needs about ten billion euros in savings and new revenue by twenty twenty-nine. Vooruit wants the wealthiest Belgians to contribute more, and smaller cuts to healthcare and pensions. The MR, Les Engagés and CD&V have accepted De Wever's framework. And the pressure from outside isn't easing. IANS recalls that the European Commission's autumn forecast, published last week, expects Belgium's deficit to rise from five point two per cent of the economy this year to five point five next year if nothing changes.

Over to the European Union, where the European Parliament is meeting in Strasbourg this week. According to the agenda published by the liberal Renew Europe group, members debate the rule of law in Hungary this morning. Renew says Hungary's new government has made progress, and that some of the EU's budget restrictions on the country have been lifted. On Wednesday, there's a debate on financing Ukraine. Renew says Ukraine needs about seventy billion euros for twenty twenty-seven, and it supports the Commission's proposed loan of forty-five billion, which we covered last week. EU leaders also meet next week on the long-term EU budget, where Parliament has a veto.

Trade news from the weekend, too. According to the newsletter Ten Things, the EU and Indonesia signed a free trade and investment agreement on Saturday, after about ten years of talks. The Commission says tariffs will be removed on about ninety-eight and a half per cent of EU goods sold to Indonesia, and it projects EU exports there to grow by nearly forty per cent over fifteen years. That's a projection, and the deal still needs the approval of the European Parliament.

And to the world, starting with Poland. Ten Things reports that two missiles and a drone came down in south-east Poland during Russian strikes on Ukraine on Friday. One was a Russian Kh-101 cruise missile. Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk called it a provocation, with no injuries or damage reported. President Trump called it a mistake. NATO's Secretary General, Mark Rutte, pledged to defend every inch of Polish territory.

In Washington, the Director of National Intelligence, Jay Clayton, said on Sunday that Vladimir Putin is not America's friend in Ukraine. That came days after Trump called Putin a friend.

And in Libya, a Russian corvette reportedly capsized in the port of Benghazi on Sunday. Eastern Libyan authorities blame Ukrainian sea drones. According to Ten Things, that has not been independently confirmed, and the United Nations mission in Libya has called for an independent investigation.

## Energy
Over to energy, and to Belgium first, where the cold is showing up in the price of power.

According to the price comparison site Selectra, which publishes the Belgian day-ahead price every day, today's average price on the Belgian power exchange is about twenty-four point seven cents per kilowatt-hour. That's roughly two hundred and forty-seven euros per megawatt-hour. On Friday, we reported a price of around two hundred and ten. The cheapest hour today, according to Selectra, was between three and four in the morning. If you have a dynamic contract, that's when to run the dishwasher.

The reason sits mostly in the gas market. Over to Europe. Bloomberg reported on Monday that European gas surged as a cold snap threatens tight inventories. The British energy broker Prestige Business Solutions gives the details in its market report. The main Dutch gas contract, the TTF, which sets the benchmark price for much of Europe, settled at about eighty-eight euros per megawatt-hour. Two things pushed it up. First, temperatures were forecast at three to four degrees below normal for the time of year. Second, Norway, Europe's largest gas supplier, had an unplanned outage at its Åsgard field. On Friday, Norwegian gas flows fell to their lowest level since late September. They have since recovered, after maintenance at the giant Troll field ended.

The bigger worry is storage. According to Prestige, the EU's gas stores are about seventy per cent full. That's more than eleven percentage points below a year ago, and more than seventeen below the five-year average. And with the cold, Europe is already taking more gas out than it puts in. As we reported yesterday, Goldman Sachs expects the gas price to fall sharply next year. But this winter, Europe starts from a thinner cushion.

Power prices next door followed. Prestige puts German power for December at about two hundred and twenty-five euros per megawatt-hour, and French power at about two hundred and ten, both up more than six per cent in a day. The carbon price went the other way, down to about eighty-one euros per tonne. That's the price companies pay for each tonne of carbon dioxide they emit under the EU's emissions trading system. Prestige also points to the risks further afield: reports of Houthi strikes on a Saudi refinery over the weekend, and fewer tankers passing through the Strait of Hormuz than in October. Both keep traders nervous about liquefied gas supplies from the Gulf.

Staying with power, but back to AI. Data centres are becoming one of the biggest new sources of electricity demand. The Neuron, citing Fortune, reports that SpaceX plans seventy-four billion dollars of AI data centres in Texas and Arizona. The Arizona campus alone would need about six gigawatts at full build-out. That's more than the entire nuclear fleet Belgium still runs today. Residents and local officials want the developer, not ordinary bill payers, to cover the cost of the grid upgrades. And as Axios reports, Meta's growth in the United States leans partly on new gas plants, including seven gas units totalling more than five thousand megawatts planned by the utility Entergy in Louisiana.

That question, who pays for the grid that data centres need, is one European grid operators and regulators will face as well.

And one date to keep in mind. The Belgian state and Engie are still aiming to agree the main terms for the state's takeover of Engie's Belgian nuclear business by the twelfth of December. With the federal government now giving itself until New Year's Eve to survive, that deal may need a government to sign it.

## Term of the day
Today's term is model router. A model router is a service that sits between your app and many different AI models. You send it one request, and it decides which model, and which company hosting that model, should answer. Think of a travel booking site. You ask for a flight to Lisbon, and the site checks many airlines and picks one for you. You never deal with the airline directly.

It came up twice today. OpenAI now routes ChatGPT users to different models depending on which tab they are in. And OpenRouter, one of the best-known routers, removed three hosting providers after researchers found leaked secrets. Routers make it easy to switch models and cut costs. The trade-off is trust: every hop is another company that sees your users' data.
