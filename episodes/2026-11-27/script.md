## Intro
Good morning Thomas, it's Friday the 27th of November. Here's your Daily Brief. Today: Volvo starts teaching humanoid robots to work in its factory in Ghent. In Belgium, the coalition parties circle Vooruit's two conditions ahead of a meeting on Monday. And EU leaders leave Brussels without a deal on the long-term budget.

## AI
We start with AI, and with a story that's very close to home: robots that look a bit like people, learning to work on a Belgian assembly line.

On Thursday, Volvo Cars and the Swedish technology group Hexagon announced that Hexagon's humanoid robot, called AEON, is now being trained inside Volvo's plant in Ghent. According to VRT News, it's the first Volvo factory to test humanoids. AEON is about one metre sixty-five tall and weighs around sixty kilos. It rolls on wheels rather than walking on legs, and it sees with cameras and sensors. It can carry up to ten kilos.

The first job is modest. The robot has to pick up components and place them precisely into two holders. Later, Volvo wants it to handle more kinds of parts, move around the factory, and talk to the automated systems that are already there. More robots are due to arrive in the first half of next year for a wider pilot. The companies say they are aiming for real production use from the middle of twenty twenty-seven. That's a target, not a promise.

Why a humanoid, when car factories are already full of robots? Volvo's answer, as reported by VRT and New Mobility News, is that its classic robot arms are great at doing the same thing in one fixed spot. But some tiring, awkward jobs need a worker who can move between stations and fit into a line built for people. Volvo's head of manufacturing, Magnus Nilsson, put it this way: "AEON's humanoid design means it can fit into our production as it is today." In other words, Volvo doesn't have to rebuild the factory around the robot.

How does the robot learn? New Mobility News says it uses imitation learning: the robot watches how a task is done and copies it. The Next Web describes Volvo staff teaching the robots by demonstration. And importantly, Volvo's own team does the training, together with Hexagon. So Volvo is buying the robot, but keeping the know-how about its own tasks in-house.

A plant spokesperson, Barbara Blomme, told New Mobility News that staff are enthusiastic and already used to working alongside robots. There's no reaction from the unions in the reports so far. And several things are still unknown: how many robots there will be, what they cost, and how often the first task actually succeeds. The tech site i10x points out that the ten-kilo limit rules out most heavy assembly work. And Ghent is not alone. The Next Web notes that BMW has already piloted the same robot in Leipzig, so carmakers across Europe are running similar experiments, each hoping to learn first what these machines can really do on a busy line.

Now, three shorter stories. First, Europe and money. On Wednesday, the European Commission confirmed plans for a Sovereign Tech Equity Fund, aiming at around fifty billion euros, to take direct stakes in European AI and quantum computing companies. According to The Next Web, the goal is to make bigger, riskier and longer-term bets than existing EU schemes, so that promising firms don't have to look to American investors to grow. The Commission's tech chief, Henna Virkkunen, said no company should have to leave Europe for lack of finance. For now this is a proposal and a target, not a fixed budget. Parliament and the member states still have their say.

Second, OpenAI is changing how it counts usage. From December, subscribers on the Plus, Pro and Business plans will draw ChatGPT conversations and Codex coding tasks from one shared allowance, measured in tokens, which are the small chunks of text a model reads and writes. TechCrunch reports that heavy users of both may hit their limit sooner. Quick replies from the light Instant mode won't count. OpenAI hasn't published the size of the allowance, which is exactly what critics on developer forums complain about.

And third, regulation in America. The Neuron, citing NBC News, reports that New York's governor, Kathy Hochul, has signed a law banning the making and selling of toys powered by AI chatbots for five years.

So why does the Volvo story matter for a product manager? Because it's a textbook case of fitting new technology into an existing workflow instead of redesigning everything around it. Start with one narrow, measurable task, own the training data for your own process, and only widen the scope once the first job reliably works.

## Politics
Over to politics, and to Belgium first, where the government is still standing, but only just.

The day after Conner Rousseau's press conference, the debate moved to the Chamber. During Thursday's question time, VRT News reports, the budget minister, Vincent Van Peteghem of CD and V, warned that the financial markets will judge Belgium on actions, not on statements in the press. The finance minister, Jan Jambon of the N-VA, said the eyes of the markets are on the country. The MR president, Georges-Louis Bouchez, rejected extra taxes on multinationals and millionaires and said growth was the answer. He also said no skipped index jump is planned, and that a fall of the government should be avoided at all costs. From the opposition, the PS president, Paul Magnette, said there was no alternative to making the richest contribute.

What about Monday? According to government sources quoted by VRT, Prime Minister Bart De Wever has called the party presidents together on Monday at two in the afternoon. Vooruit says it will only come if the others confirm its two conditions in advance: a contribution from the largest fortunes, and no touching the wage index, unless the social partners agree on a change. Vooruit's minister Rob Beenders said the party wants a fair budget and is waiting for an answer before Monday.

Is there movement? On French-language television on Thursday evening, the deputy prime minister Maxime Prévot of Les Engagés said no party had refused to talk about Vooruit's demands. But he added that Vooruit can't demand that everyone signs before Monday. The former Vooruit deputy prime minister Johan Vande Lanotte told VRT that respecting Vooruit's red lines is the only way to a deal, though he doubts the coalition will accept them. He also stressed that if Vooruit leaves, new elections don't follow automatically. Earlier in the afternoon, the opposition tried to summon De Wever to the Chamber, because he skipped question time to attend the EU summit. The majority voted that motion down.

Which brings us to the European Union. EU leaders left Brussels on Thursday night without a deal on the next long-term budget, for twenty twenty-eight to twenty thirty-four. EUobserver reports that the President of the European Council, António Costa, had proposed cutting about two hundred and four billion euros from the European Commission's original plan. That pleased nobody. The German Chancellor, Friedrich Merz, said the cut would have to be bigger, by several hundred billion. The Netherlands, Denmark and Sweden also want a smaller budget, and EUobserver describes Belgium as an associate member of that group. On the other side, President Emmanuel Macron called the cuts a strategic mistake and said France won't back any more. Poland's Donald Tusk said his camp is not backing down. Leaders also failed to settle a forty-five billion euro loan for Ukraine's budget next year, with Hungary, Czechia and Slovakia opposed, according to Euronews. The next attempt is the summit on the seventeenth and eighteenth of December. Macron warned that the end of December is the absolute limit for him.

To the world. In Washington, the Trump administration announced new tariffs on Wednesday on fourteen economies it accuses of excess production capacity, the Associated Press reports. The European Union could face fifteen per cent, and China twenty per cent, possibly doubling to forty. The EU rejected the idea that Europe is a source of overcapacity. China called it a false premise and promised necessary measures. The tariffs are expected to take effect early next year.

On Ukraine, the date and format of the next round of peace talks are still open. President Zelensky says Kyiv is ready for a four-sided meeting including Europe, and Ursula von der Leyen says a strong European delegation will take part. The Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov has said European involvement is not on Moscow's agenda.

And finally, Spain votes on Sunday. The four main candidates, Pedro Sánchez, Alberto Núñez Feijóo, Santiago Abascal and Ada Colau, held their final televised debate on Thursday night, according to the site In Spain News. Campaigning ends tonight at midnight, and Spanish law has banned new polls since Tuesday. A majority needs a hundred and seventy-six of three hundred and fifty seats.

## Energy
Over to energy, and to Belgium first, where the cold is showing up clearly in the price of power.

According to the price trackers energyprices dot be and CallMePower, Thursday's day-ahead price on the Belgian power exchange averaged about two hundred and ninety-one euros per megawatt-hour. That's almost fifteen per cent higher than on Wednesday. The day-ahead price is set at an auction the day before, for each hour or quarter-hour of the next day. The most expensive hour came between five and six in the evening, at about three hundred and eighty euros, when people get home, turn on the heating and the lights, and the sun has gone. The cheapest was in the middle of the night, at just under two hundred.

Zoom out, and the picture is even starker. CallMePower puts the average for November so far at about two hundred and thirty-four euros per megawatt-hour. That's roughly two point seven times higher than November last year. For households on a variable contract, wholesale prices like these feed into the bill with a delay of a month or so.

The main reason is gas. Trading Economics reports that the Dutch TTF contract, the European benchmark, ended Thursday at about eighty-seven to eighty-eight euros per megawatt-hour, more or less flat on the day, and still around three times its level a year ago. European gas storage has slipped below sixty-eight per cent full, against a five-year average of about eighty-six per cent for the time of year. Forecasters expect cold spells in north-western and central Europe into early December. And there's a second cost pushing up power prices: according to the financial news service Wallstreetcn, the price of an EU carbon permit, which power plants must buy for every tonne of carbon dioxide they emit, rose almost three per cent on Thursday, to about eighty-four euros a tonne.

Staying with Belgium, and with nuclear. On Tuesday evening, the energy minister, Mathieu Bihet of the MR, told La Libre that the barriers to new nuclear are gone, and that a company could in principle decide tomorrow to invest in a small modular reactor, a factory-built nuclear plant of up to about three hundred megawatts. His example was a chemicals company powering its own site. The safety rules already exist, he said. Large plants of a thousand megawatts or more would still need the state to lead. The occasion was a new barometer from the employers' federation FEB, the technology federation Agoria, the nuclear industry forum and the research centre SCK CEN in Mol. It counts fifty-one steps needed to build a new plant, and says twenty-two are done or under way. Bihet admitted the main worry for business: that policy might be reversed again in three years.

And the bigger nuclear file has a deadline. The Belgian state and Engie are aiming to agree the main terms of the state's takeover of Engie's Belgian reactors by the twelfth of December. That's fifteen days from now. That covers all seven of Engie's Belgian reactors. The two sides have already pushed the target date back this autumn. And the talks continue even as the government's own future hangs on Monday's meeting. Vooruit, for its part, has said it backs the Prime Minister on this file, so a deal on nuclear is not, for now, part of the budget fight.

## Term of the day
Today's term is imitation learning.

Imitation learning means teaching a machine by showing it how a task is done, rather than writing out every step in code. The machine watches examples, often recorded by a person guiding it, and learns to copy the pattern.

Think of how you learned to tie your shoelaces. Nobody gave you a manual with angles and distances. Someone showed you, slowly, a few times, and you copied them until it worked on your own. Robots learn in a similar way, just from many more examples.

It came up today because that's how Volvo and Hexagon are training the AEON humanoid robots in Ghent. Volvo staff demonstrate the task, placing parts into holders, and the robot learns to repeat it. The catch is that it only learns what it has been shown, so every new task needs new demonstrations.
