## AI
We start with AI, and with a company deciding that its software should be used by robots as well as by people.

At its MAX conference in Miami Beach on Tuesday, Adobe announced that outside AI agents will be able to work directly inside Photoshop, Illustrator, Premiere and After Effects. According to The Next Web, these agents will change the real project files on your own computer, and the layers, vectors and video sequences stay fully editable. That's the key difference with most AI image tools, which hand you a flat picture you can't easily adjust. The connection runs through MCP, short for Model Context Protocol, an open standard that lets AI agents use other software. More on that in our term of the day.

Adobe's demo video shows ChatGPT working in a beta version of Photoshop. It took a party poster for one DJ and made versions for two more, each with a new photo, name, city and date. It reused the layers already in the file, translated the São Paulo poster into Portuguese, and resized all three into two more formats. The result, according to The Next Web, was nine editable artboards in just over nine minutes. A person can step in at any point and finish the job by hand.

Now the caveats. This is a preview, not a product. Adobe says the integrations enter a private beta in early twenty twenty-seven. FourWeekMBA notes that the announcements give no price, no date for general availability and no list of supported agents. An Adobe vice-president told TechCrunch that the company is still deciding which capabilities to expose.

Adobe is pushing in two directions at once. Outward, it's placing its own tools inside rival AI assistants, such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Microsoft's Copilot. Inward, it's letting those outside agents act on its native files. On top of that, its own assistant, the Adobe Creative Agent, is now in beta in Premiere, Illustrator, InDesign and After Effects. You describe a goal, such as applying the same colour correction across a whole video sequence, and it carries out the steps. And Adobe widened its partnership with Google. Acrobat is coming to Gmail and Google Drive, and Google's newer image and video models are coming to Photoshop and Premiere. No financial terms were disclosed.

Staying with the AI industry, and with a disclosure first: this podcast is written with Claude, which is made by Anthropic, so I'll stick closely to what the sources say. Anthropic has launched the roadshow for its planned stock market listing. A roadshow is the series of meetings in which a company pitches its shares to big investors before they go on sale. According to Anthropic's own newsroom, it started on Tuesday, following the registration filing with the American markets regulator in late October. The newsletter The Neuron reports a price range of seventy to seventy-eight dollars a share, for a listing on the Nasdaq exchange. At the top of that range, the offering would raise about sixty-four billion dollars before costs. The shares can't be sold yet, because the regulator hasn't declared the registration effective.

One more item, on AI checking software. According to Wired, as summarised by The Neuron, a Google DeepMind tool called AlphaProof Code helped to formally verify key security properties of the encryption behind the messaging app Signal, in about four weeks. Formal verification means proving mathematically that code does what it promises, rather than just testing it. The researchers stress the limits. The proof covers specific properties, not every part of Signal or every possible attack. But they also confirmed that the system rejects older versions with known bugs.

So why does Adobe's move matter for a product manager? Because the user of your product may increasingly be an agent acting for a person. Adobe is betting that its moat is the editable file and the workflow around it, not the buttons on the screen. The question for any product team is which of your capabilities you'd expose to agents, and how you'd keep control, quality and pricing when you do.

## Politics
Over to politics, and to Belgium first, where the Prime Minister has now heard everyone.

On Wednesday, Bart De Wever received Vincent Van Peteghem, vice-prime minister for CD&V, at his office in the Wetstraat. According to VRT News, the meeting lasted about an hour. Van Peteghem said nothing on the way in or the way out. It was the last of his one-to-one talks with his vice-prime ministers. All the vice-prime ministers came with their chief of staff, the regional broadcaster BX1 notes, except David Clarinval of the MR, who brought his party president, Georges-Louis Bouchez.

So what comes next? Nobody knows yet. BX1 reports that De Wever hasn't said how he'll proceed. Speaking to the news agency Belga on the sidelines of the Armistice Day commemorations at the Congress Column, he said a truce in the government was still awaited. VRT reports that the coalition partners might not sit together again before the weekend. The positions remain far apart. On Tuesday, Clarinval said the Prime Minister must decide whether to stay within the coalition agreement or renegotiate it, because the MR caps new taxes at one point one billion euros. That's against a total effort of around ten billion. On Monday the sixteenth, De Wever must report back to King Philippe. VRT sketches three scenarios: an agreement, another extension, or the fall of the government.

To keep his diary clear, De Wever is skipping the informal meeting of European leaders in Dublin, which starts today. VRT called that a surprising choice.

That brings us to the European Union, where there's a budget deal, at least. The European Parliament and the member states have agreed on the Union's annual budget for twenty twenty-seven. According to EU Business, which reported the Council's announcement, it sets commitments, the promises to spend, at nearly two hundred billion euros, and actual payments at about two hundred and eleven and a half billion. It includes six hundred and fifty million euros for the Ukraine support loan, around three billion for security and defence, and almost five point eight billion for migration and border management. The deal isn't final yet. Both institutions have fourteen days to approve it formally, and the Council is expected to endorse it on the twenty-third of November. On Tuesday we heard that the toughest point was who pays the interest on the Union's debt. The reports I could check don't say how that was settled.

Then they fly to Ireland to brief Zelenskyy, who is attending the European leaders' meeting in Dublin. Ukraine also says it's still waiting for Russia's answer on a ceasefire for grain shipping in the Black Sea.

## Energy
Over to energy, and to Belgium first, where there's a queue being cleaned up.

According to the news agency Belga, Elia, the operator of Belgium's high-voltage grid, and the energy regulator, the CREG, have refined how they assess requests to connect to the grid. In recent years, companies have sharply increased those requests, especially for big battery parks and data centres. But grid capacity is limited, so Elia has to study whether each new connection can actually be served. That created a long waiting list, and for some companies, the wait put their investment at risk.

Part of the problem, Belga reports, is what's called phantom studies. Some companies file several requests for the same project, file for very uncertain plans, or secure a good site on the grid to sell it on later. The new approach filters those out more effectively. Figures released by the CREG on Tuesday show the waiting list is slowly getting shorter, and Elia expects to clear the historical backlog completely by March.

Staying with grids, but going European. Google's charity arm, Google dot org, has given two and a half million dollars to LF Energy, the Linux Foundation's group for open-source energy software. According to Renewable Energy Magazine, the two-year grant should speed up free tools for modelling, forecasting and sharing grid data, so that AI can actually be used on it. Several of these tools are already used by grid operators in France and the Netherlands. The focus is on helping smaller operators that can't build such tools themselves.

Now to the power markets, where the cold, dark weather we discussed on Monday is biting hard in central and eastern Europe. According to Balkan Green Energy News, day-ahead power prices, the wholesale prices for the next day, reached around four hundred and sixty euros per megawatt-hour in Poland and above four hundred in Romania and Bulgaria earlier this week. Evening peaks passed six hundred euros in several markets, the highest since twenty twenty-two. The reasons are familiar. Gas, which sets the electricity price in many hours, is expensive because of the war in the Persian Gulf. Gas storage is low, the lowest for the season since twenty thirteen, according to the International Energy Agency. And wind and solar output has been weak. On top of that, low river levels have cut hydropower and limited the cooling water for some power plants. The same article cites the analysts at Kpler, who expect gas plants to run most of the time this winter, more than seventy per cent of the time in Germany and sixty-five per cent in France. That's a forecast, but it matters for us, because Belgium imports a lot of power from both neighbours, and, as we heard on Tuesday, Belgian prices already passed five hundred euros per megawatt-hour at Monday's evening peak.

Finally, the global climate summit, COP31, in Antalya in Türkiye, where world leaders spoke on Wednesday. According to the World Resources Institute, a group of countries endorsed the Turkish presidency's electrification pledge, which aims to raise electricity's share of the world's final energy use from about twenty-three per cent today to thirty-five per cent by twenty thirty-five. The institute didn't say how many countries signed, or whether the EU or Belgium is among them. It's a voluntary pledge, so the real test is national plans. RTÉ, the Irish broadcaster, reports that more than a hundred and eighty large companies, including IKEA, Volvo and Siemens, have backed faster electrification. And the Global Carbon Project, a team of more than a hundred and forty scientists, projects that carbon dioxide emissions from fossil fuels will fall by about zero point four per cent this year. That's a forecast, but if it holds, it would be the first yearly decline since twenty twenty.

## Term of the day
Today's term is MCP, which stands for Model Context Protocol. It's an open standard that lets an AI assistant connect to other software in a uniform way. The software describes what it can do, such as "open this file" or "resize this layer", and the AI can then call those actions itself. Think of it like the universal plug sockets in a hotel that work with any charger. Instead of every app building a special adapter for every AI assistant, they all agree on one socket. It came up today because Adobe plans to use MCP to let outside agents, such as ChatGPT, work directly in Photoshop and Premiere. For a product manager, offering an MCP connection is becoming a bit like offering an interface for developers used to be: a way for other tools to build on yours.
