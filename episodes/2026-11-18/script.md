## Intro
Good morning Thomas, it's Wednesday the eighteenth of November. Today: OpenAI launches two new models, and some of its most loyal users are not impressed. In Belgium, Frank Vandenbroucke goes on television to blame the Prime Minister for the failed budget talks. And in energy, gas prices climb again as a cold spell approaches and Europe's storage sits well below normal.

## AI
We start with AI, and with a launch that has split opinion more sharply than any OpenAI release in a long time.

On Tuesday, OpenAI released two new models, GPT-6.2 Astra and GPT-6.2 Sol. According to VentureBeat, Astra is the heavyweight, aimed at demanding work like large changes to a code base, building working web apps, or writing documents that follow a company's own templates. Sol is the cheaper option, meant for routine coding, writing and multi-step workflows. Both can take in about a million tokens at once. A token is roughly three quarters of a word, so that's enough for several long books in a single request.

On price, OpenAI is getting more aggressive. Astra costs eight dollars per million tokens going in and forty dollars per million coming out. VentureBeat says that's twenty per cent cheaper than the previous Astra, and exactly level with Anthropic's Claude Fable five point two, which launched eight days earlier. Sol stays at two dollars in and ten out. Batch processing, where you send work to be done later rather than straight away, costs half. A faster mode costs double. Both are available now in ChatGPT and in OpenAI's coding tool Codex for paying users, and through the programming interface for developers.

OpenAI's own benchmarks look good. Astra beats its predecessor on all nine of the tests OpenAI chose to show, and Sol on eight of nine. But the independent picture is more mixed. On Artificial Analysis's coding agent index, Astra was flat, and Sol actually dropped three points. On the same firm's general intelligence index, Astra ranks second, behind Claude Fable.

And then there's the reaction from people who use these models every day. The investor Matt Shumer said the models were, in his words, "essentially unusable", and that he would not publish his usual detailed review. He said that when he let a Claude model supervise Astra as a helper, Claude kept redoing Astra's work. The developer Theo Browne was harsher still, calling Astra the worst model OpenAI has ever released. Dan Shipper, who runs the publication Every, said Astra matched its predecessor on most coding tests, but often stopped early on full end-to-end builds. And the Wharton professor Ethan Mollick said it was the first OpenAI frontier release since GPT-4.5 that he did not see as a clear step forward.

To be fair, there was praise too. Several testers liked Astra's work on animation, video editing and visual effects. Browne credited it with catching code problems other models missed. And OpenAI says many of its own staff use Sol as their default.

Staying with the big AI labs, this is the day Anthropic is expected to set the price of its stock market listing. According to Yahoo Finance, citing CNBC, the shares are being offered at between seventy and seventy-eight dollars each, on the Nasdaq exchange under the ticker A N T, with trading due to start tomorrow. At the top of that range, the listing would raise around sixty-four billion dollars, and value the company at roughly a trillion dollars. These are the terms on offer, not a final price. That comes tonight.

And the filing shows who will own Anthropic afterwards. According to TechCrunch, Amazon will hold about fifteen per cent and Google close to fourteen, so together nearly thirty per cent. Much of that comes from converting earlier loans into shares that carry no vote. On top of that, seven suppliers of chips and computing power, including Nvidia, Microsoft, Broadcom and Google, have agreed to buy twenty-six billion dollars of non-voting shares at the listing price. Anthropic has also committed to spend more than a hundred billion dollars on Amazon's cloud over the next decade. In other words, the companies that sell Anthropic its computing power are also among its biggest owners.

So why does the Astra launch matter for a product manager? Because it shows that a model's launch benchmarks and its real behaviour inside your product can be two very different things. Before switching models, run your own tests on your own tasks, and keep the option to route different jobs to different providers.

## Politics
Over to politics, and to Belgium first, where the blame game has started, even as the clock keeps ticking.

As we covered yesterday, King Philippe gave Prime Minister Bart De Wever one more week, until next Monday, to find a budget agreement. On Tuesday, the Vooruit vice-prime minister, Frank Vandenbroucke, went on television to explain why his party said no. According to Business AM, he told the channel VTM and the programme Terzake that De Wever had put a rigid take-it-or-leave-it proposal on the table. After hours of talks, Vandenbroucke said, he was told the terms could not be changed, so he rejected it. A budget deal is essential, he said, but it cannot be reached by forcing a yes-or-no vote on a pre-written text.

He went further. Vandenbroucke said that his views as a moderate Flemish social democrat count for less than those of the MR leader, Georges-Louis Bouchez, whom he described in sharply personal terms. And he named the issues where Vooruit is holding firm. He says he could accept significant savings on long-term sickness, about eight hundred million euros on top of the existing two point six billion, according to The Brussels Times. But he refuses measures that would mean further losses for Flemish teachers and pensioners through the index, the system that automatically raises wages and benefits with prices. And he criticised tax advantages for professional footballers, worth about two hundred and fifty million euros, which he called Father Christmas politics.

At the same time, Vooruit is not pushing for an exit. Vandenbroucke said he does not want early elections, and does not want a coalition with the French-speaking socialists of the PS. The party chairman, Conner Rousseau, said Vooruit wants an agreement, and will come back to the table in a constructive spirit if invited. The timetable for the coming days is unclear.

The other side sees it differently. On Monday, the N-VA chair, Valerie Van Peel, told VRT News the proposal on the table was balanced and fair, and warned the parties were at the edge of an abyss. In opposition, Vlaams Belang has tabled a motion of no confidence. The workers' party PVDA is calling on people to join a trade union demonstration in Brussels next Monday, the same day De Wever is due back at the palace.

Over to the European Union, where Europe is edging closer to a seat at the table on Ukraine. According to Al Jazeera, Ukraine held online talks on Monday with the American envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, and with European representatives. President Zelenskyy said France, Germany, Britain, Italy and Poland took full part, and would be represented the same way in future meetings with Russian negotiators. The Kyiv Independent reports that the German Chancellor, Friedrich Merz, is expected to host a summit of the so-called Coalition of the Willing in Berlin tomorrow, to agree on the next steps. But the two sides tell different stories about Moscow. Kushner says Russia is open to Europeans joining. The Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov says Russia has not agreed to widen the talks beyond Russia, Ukraine and the United States. Zelenskyy also said a prisoner exchange of up to thirteen hundred people could happen in the coming weeks. The Kremlin spokesman, Dmitry Peskov, said Russia wants a lasting peace rather than a temporary ceasefire that would let Ukraine recover, and warned that the negotiations could take considerable time. Ireland's foreign minister, Helen McEntee, welcomed the agreement on European representation, but said Russia has clearly not changed its position. Ahead of Monday's meeting of European Union foreign ministers in Brussels, she pointed to a hundred and thirteen new sanctions on Russia, meant to keep up the pressure.

And to the wider world, where the diplomacy is running alongside some of the heaviest fighting in months. According to Al Jazeera, Russia's defence ministry called Monday night's Ukrainian drone attack the biggest on Russia since the full-scale war began, with the Crimea Bridge struck again. In Kyiv, the mayor reported possible hits on two bridges across the Dnipro. And President Trump said he would discuss the war with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin in China. The Kyiv Independent reports that this meeting is planned for tomorrow in Shenzhen, the same day as Berlin.

## Energy
Over to energy, and to Belgium first, where the budget crisis is still casting a shadow over the nuclear file.

As we've followed, the federal government and Engie are negotiating a state takeover of all of Engie's nuclear activities in Belgium. According to World Nuclear News, that covers the reactors, the staff, and the costs of eventually dismantling the plants. The current target for an agreement in principle is the twelfth of December, after two missed deadlines, and the parties say the main gap is still the price. Only two reactors are running today, Doel four and Tihange three, both extended to twenty thirty-five. With the Prime Minister fully absorbed by the budget, that December date is now less than four weeks away.

Why does this matter right now? Because Belgium has been leaning heavily on its neighbours for electricity. According to The Brussels Times, citing the Federal Economy Ministry, Belgium imported a record amount of power in the first half of this year, with a net import balance of twelve and a half terawatt-hours. In September, domestic production fell to its lowest monthly level since records began in two thousand and eight, mainly because both reactors were offline for maintenance. They are back now, but it shows how exposed the country is when supply is tight.

And supply across Europe is tight. On Tuesday, the Dutch gas benchmark, known as TTF, rose about two per cent to around eighty-six euros per megawatt-hour, according to OilPrice. That's after a seven per cent drop on Monday, and nearly three times the price of a year ago. Two things are driving it. First, shipping through the Strait of Hormuz is still disrupted, and liquefied gas leaving the Gulf is estimated at about a third of pre-war levels. Second, forecasts show a cold spell across most of Europe from around Friday, which will push up demand for heating and power. Europe's gas storage is only about seventy per cent full, compared with roughly ninety per cent in earlier years. And Germany's storage association, INES, has warned that supply may not be fully secured in extremely cold weather. That's a warning, not a forecast of shortages, but it is worth watching as winter arrives. For Belgian households, it matters too. As we reported last week, new variable contracts in Flanders are already at their highest prices since early twenty twenty-three.

Finally, the climate summit, COP31 in Antalya, which is due to end on Friday. As we reported yesterday, a first draft deal includes wording on phasing out fossil fuels and a call for wealthy countries to reach net zero by twenty thirty. According to Al Jazeera, the European Union's climate envoy, Wopke Hoekstra, now says the draft needs significant change and could make the talks untenable. An unnamed European official accused a bloc including Saudi Arabia and India of trying to shift obligations onto developed countries. There's also a row over when the next major report from the United Nations climate science panel should come out. Saudi Arabia says it is not trying to delay climate science. Not everyone is gloomy, though. Brazil's environment minister, João Paulo Capobianco, said early red lines are easy to draw, and that deadlocks can often be solved by adjusting the wording. Antigua and Barbuda's minister, Michael Joseph, said consensus on net zero could be very difficult, but he would like a firm commitment to phase out fossil fuels. Australia, which is steering the talks, expects at least one more draft this week.

## Term of the day
Today's term is sub-agent.

A sub-agent is an AI model that works under the direction of another AI model. The main agent breaks a big task into smaller pieces, hands some of them to helpers, and then checks and combines what comes back. Think of a head chef in a busy kitchen. The chef doesn't chop every onion, but tells the sous-chefs what to prepare, tastes the result, and sends back anything that isn't right.

It came up today because of the reaction to OpenAI's new Astra model. The investor Matt Shumer said that when he used Astra as a sub-agent, with a Claude model acting as the supervisor, Claude kept redoing Astra's work. For product teams, this setup is increasingly common. You might use an expensive model to plan and check, and cheaper models to do the bulk of the work.
