## Intro
Good morning Thomas, it's Thursday the twenty-ninth of October. Here's your Daily Brief. Today: OpenAI reportedly plans to buy Google's AI chips, on the same day Google's cloud business doubled in size. Israel's vote count puts Netanyahu's camp in front, but still short of a majority. And European gas crosses one hundred euros per megawatt-hour for the first time since twenty twenty-two.

## AI
We start with AI, and with the hardware underneath it.

On Tuesday evening, the tech publication The Information reported that OpenAI plans to buy Google's AI chips, citing a person familiar with the matter. Reuters picked up the story. Google calls these chips TPUs, or tensor processing units. They are Google's own alternative to the graphics chips from Nvidia that most AI companies train and run their models on. No price, quantity or delivery date was given. OpenAI declined to comment, and Google didn't respond to Reuters. So treat this as a report, not a confirmed deal.

The detail that matters is the word buy. According to Decrypt, OpenAI has been renting Google's chips since June last year, paying by the hour through Google's cloud. Buying means owning the hardware. Decrypt also points to an OpenAI job advert for an engineer to optimise its frontier models for TPUs, which suggests real deployment is being planned. This would add a fourth source of computing power. OpenAI already buys huge volumes from Nvidia, has a deal with AMD tied to six gigawatts of chips, and is building its own inference chip with Broadcom, due to start deployment by the end of this year.

Then, on Wednesday, Google's parent company Alphabet reported its results for the third quarter, and they explain why this matters to Google. According to 9to5Google, Alphabet's revenue rose twenty-five per cent to one hundred and twenty-seven point six billion dollars. Google Cloud more than doubled, to thirty point seven billion dollars, growth of one hundred and three per cent. Chief executive Sundar Pichai spoke of strong demand for TPU systems across a wide range of customers. Google's own models are busy too. The Gemini app has passed one billion users, and Google's model programming interfaces now process nearly thirty-two billion tokens per minute, more than four times the rate a year ago. A token is a small chunk of text, roughly three quarters of a word. Investing dot com reports that profit came in below expectations, and the shares fell about ten per cent in after-hours trading.

So the picture is this. Google is now both OpenAI's rival in models and, potentially, one of its suppliers of hardware. Anthropic signed a large deal for Google's chips in April. Chips are becoming a market with several real options, not a single supplier.

Next, a lesson in what can go wrong when you rent AI from someone else. On Monday, Cloudflare published a detailed disclosure about its Workers AI service, which lets developers call open models over the internet. Between the twenty-seventh of August and the ninth of September, some customers using the GLM five point three model received answers containing data from other customers' requests. Cloudflare counted one hundred and fifty-five affected responses. All of them came from extremely long prompts, of more than six hundred and eighty thousand tokens.

The cause was two problems stacked together. The model was advertised with a context window, the amount of text it can read at once, of about one million tokens. But the memory set aside for that work only held about six hundred and eighty thousand. Cloudflare says it knew about the gap but didn't treat it as a problem, because its long tests came back without errors. Then a bug in the open-source serving software, called SGLang, meant that a request too big to fit was not stopped. It carried on and read leftover memory from earlier requests. The customer got a success message instead of an error.

A security researcher at the firm Knostic reported it on the eighth of September. Cloudflare says it reproduced the problem within twenty-five minutes, took the model offline the next day, and fixed it within hours. It found no sign of deliberate exploitation.

One shorter item. Earlier this week, OpenAI released GPT six point one Terra, its mid-range model. According to DataCamp, it costs one dollar per million input tokens and five dollars per million output tokens, half the price of the model it replaces. OpenAI says it is better at staying within the scope of a task and at telling users when it is stuck. Those are OpenAI's own figures, not independent tests.

Why does the Cloudflare story matter for a product manager? When your feature runs on someone else's model, keeping each customer's data separate is a property of your vendor, not something you control. Ask suppliers how they isolate customers, and test the edges of what they advertise, like the longest inputs, not just the happy path.

## Politics
Over to politics, and to Belgium first.

The federal government's top ministers, known as the core cabinet, were due to meet again on Wednesday evening to try to close the budget. According to Belga, insiders were not expecting a breakthrough, and in the corridors people were betting on a deal early next week. At the time of writing, no agreement had been announced. Business AM sums up the plan as about ten billion euros by twenty twenty-nine: roughly four and a half billion from reforms and savings, about one and a half billion in extra revenue, and the rest from technical adjustments and postponements.

Each party still has its red lines. Business AM reports that the N-VA says it has reached its limit on concessions, particularly on business subsidies and holding companies. Vooruit opposes cuts in health care and pensions and wants more from the wealthiest. Deputy Prime Minister Frank Vandenbroucke has refused what he called rushed overnight work. CD&V opposes pension cuts. The MR rejects a broad VAT reform and prefers savings to new taxes. And Les Engagés object to the size of the health care effort. Parliament must still pass the budget laws before the end of the year.

Also in Belgium, the Federal Public Service Finance, the tax administration, was hit by a cyberattack on Wednesday. According to The Brussels Times, MyMinfin, the online tax portal, and several other services were taken offline as a precaution. The administration says there are no signs that data was stolen or compromised, but it is still investigating how far the attack reached. People who must file certain tax returns by the thirty-first of October will get an extension. The new date will be announced later.

Now to the European Union. On Wednesday, EU ambassadors held their first discussion on tougher rules for future member states. According to Euronews, the European Commission has proposed safeguards that would be written into the accession treaties of new members, with Montenegro first in line. The most serious would allow the EU to suspend a new member's voting rights if it seriously breaches EU values. The Commission suggests a fifteen-year probation period. Countries are divided. A group known as the Friends of the Western Balkans, including Italy and Austria, warns that rules this strict could slow enlargement. The Netherlands and France want stronger rule-of-law guarantees. This is a proposal under discussion, with another round expected in November.

Over to the world, and to Israel, where the picture has shifted since yesterday. Exit polls on election night suggested Gadi Eisenkot's camp was slightly ahead. But with nearly all regular votes counted, Ynet News reports that Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc has fifty-three seats, Eisenkot's bloc forty-eight, and the predominantly Arab parties nineteen. A majority needs sixty-one. Netanyahu's Likud remains the largest party with twenty-six seats. More than half a million special ballots, including many soldiers' votes, were being counted overnight and could still move seats. The Jerusalem Post reports that Eisenkot rules out serving under Netanyahu. Democrats leader Yair Golan says Netanyahu lost and wants a government supported by Arab parties, while Joint List leaders say they are ready to help remove the government. The Jerusalem Post also notes that a deadlock could lead to yet another election.

And in Ukraine, according to a roundup by Forbes, Russian missiles hit Kyiv overnight into Tuesday, killing one woman, injuring at least sixteen people, and damaging the city's Southern Bridge. Power cuts hit seven regions. A United Nations commission of inquiry concluded on Monday that Russia's attacks on Ukraine's energy system amount to war crimes and crimes against humanity. That is an investigative finding, not a court ruling. On the diplomatic side, Finland's President Alexander Stubb and Norway's Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Støre both spoke to Vladimir Putin on Tuesday, and then briefed President Zelenskyy. According to Ukraine's presidential office, they called for a de-escalation of strikes on energy infrastructure. The calls also touched on preparations for talks in November between Ukrainian, American and Russian teams. And in Brussels, Hungary again blocked the next step in Ukraine's EU membership talks.

## Energy
Over to energy, and to Belgium first, where the nuclear talks with Engie are entering their final week.

The government and Engie have until next Wednesday, the fourth of November, to agree the outline of a deal in which the state takes over all of Engie's Belgian nuclear plants. As we reported yesterday, De Tijd and L'Echo say draft legislation is ready to nationalise those plants by force if the talks fail. VRT NWS notes that the offices of the Prime Minister and Energy Minister Mathieu Bihet declined to confirm this.

The debate now is about what the plants are actually worth. L'Avenir spoke to energy economist Éric De Keuleneer, an emeritus professor at the Solvay Brussels School. He says nationalisation makes economic sense if Belgium wants nuclear to supply twenty to thirty per cent of its power, and that the state, in his words, should have done it long ago. But he warns that the plants may have no positive value at all once you count the investment to keep them running, the financial risks and the cost of dismantling them. He also questions the existing support deal for Doel four and Tihange three, which guarantees Engie a reference price of roughly ninety euros per megawatt-hour. He puts production costs closer to fifty euros, and suggests sixty to seventy. As an alternative to nationalisation, he proposes a temporary requisition, where the state runs the plants while Engie keeps ownership. Engie hasn't commented. These are one expert's views, not the government's position.

Over to Europe, and the price that drives this winter's bills. On Wednesday, the Dutch benchmark gas price, known as TTF, crossed one hundred euros per megawatt-hour for the first time since December twenty twenty-two. According to Euronews, it traded at one hundred point three seven euros in the afternoon, up more than four per cent on the day. Before the war with Iran began in late February, it was around thirty-two euros. The all-time high, in August twenty twenty-two, was about three hundred and forty.

The causes are familiar. Liquefied gas shipments from Qatar have been disrupted, and Europe's storage was only seventy-five per cent full on the twenty-first of October, against a usual target of ninety per cent by the first of November. RTÉ quotes Mathieu Utting of Rystad Energy, who says Europe has less of a storage cushion than it needs. Power prices are following. German electricity for next year rose to one hundred and fifty-nine euros per megawatt-hour. Energy Commissioner Dan Jørgensen told reporters that Europe faces another energy crisis, and that this winter will be difficult. Analysts expect high prices for at least another year. That's a forecast. RTÉ also points out that household bills lag the wholesale market, so most of the impact on consumers is expected in twenty twenty-seven.

Some suggest an old answer. The United States and Russia have reportedly discussed restarting Russian gas flows to Europe, including through the Nord Stream pipeline. On Wednesday, the European Commission shut that door. Euronews reports that the Commission's spokespeople said Nord Stream will not bring Russian gas back after the war, and that the EU's phase-out of Russian gas isn't tied to sanctions or to how the war ends. The remaining long-term contracts end by September twenty twenty-seven.

All of this lands on the table today, when EU energy ministers meet in Luxembourg. According to Spanish outlet Demócrata, no immediate decisions on prices are expected. Ministers will compare ideas to ease costs for households and business, and Spain is pushing for an EU levy on energy companies' windfall profits.

## Term of the day
Today's term is KV cache. When a language model reads your prompt, it works out a set of intermediate notes about every word, so it doesn't have to reread the whole text each time it writes the next word. Those notes are the KV cache, short for key-value cache, and they sit in the chip's fast memory. Think of a waiter jotting down your order on a notepad, instead of asking you to repeat it with every dish. The memory is reused from one request to the next, so the system must keep careful track of which pages belong to whom. It came up today because, in Cloudflare's incident, a request that was too big read pages still holding someone else's notes. That's why long context windows are expensive, and why they need careful engineering.
