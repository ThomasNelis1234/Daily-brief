## Intro

Good morning, it's Friday the twenty-third of October, and this is your Daily Brief. Today: Belgium's budget talks now aim for a deal around the second of November, a widely used AI developer tool was hijacked to steal passwords, and Europe starts talking about asking people to use less energy this winter. Let's begin.

## AI

We start with AI, and with a security story that every team building AI products should hear about.

On Wednesday, a poisoned version of LiteLLM was published. LiteLLM is a popular open-source library and proxy that lets developers call many AI model providers, such as OpenAI, Anthropic or Google, through one single interface. Think of it as a universal adapter between your app and the models. According to the security firm Mend, it was downloaded more than ninety-two million times in the past month.

Here's what happened, based on reports from The Hacker News, Mend and Chainguard. Version one point one zero seven point zero appeared on the Python package index, the main public store for Python code, just before lunchtime European time. It looked like a normal update. But the packaged files contained two hidden extras: a credential stealer, which collects cloud passwords, access keys, browser data and the stored keys for AI providers, and a backdoor that checks in every hour for new instructions. Worse, the malicious code runs whenever Python starts on an affected machine, even if the program never actually uses LiteLLM. The stolen data was sent to a look-alike web address designed to resemble the real project.

The package store quarantined the release in under two hours, and the poisoned Docker images, the ready-made containers many companies deploy, were removed in the early afternoon. LiteLLM's head of security told The Hacker News that the attack went through the GitHub accounts of three of its engineers, apparently using stolen login tokens. The company revoked all the tokens involved and, as a temporary measure, removed almost everyone from its GitHub organisation while the investigation goes on. Interestingly, the source code on GitHub itself was clean: the poison was added only to the ready-built packages, which is where most users get their software. Chainguard says its own malware scanning flagged the release sixteen minutes after it appeared. Chainguard notes that this is the second compromise of LiteLLM in seven months, after a similar incident in March. The advice from the security firms is blunt: if you installed that version, assume your credentials are gone, replace them all from a clean machine, and lock your software to the last good version.

Staying with the big players, Meta reported its third-quarter results on Wednesday evening. Revenue rose twenty-six per cent, to about sixty-four and a half billion dollars. But Meta now expects to spend between one hundred and thirty-five and one hundred and forty-five billion dollars on capital investment this year, mostly data centres for AI, and its free cash flow turned negative for the quarter. Mark Zuckerberg used the call to promote Muse, which he describes as Meta's first frontier model. According to the call transcript, Muse passed ten million users in the United States and Canada in about six weeks, and shopping is its most common use. Zuckerberg also said Meta expects to make running these models more than ten times cheaper within a year. Headcount, meanwhile, is down fifteen per cent on last year. Meta is also building an offer for businesses, with a model interface for developers, a coding tool and business agents. In short, Meta is betting that it can turn enormous spending into AI assistants that people use every day, while keeping fewer people on the payroll.

And one trend worth noting from TechCrunch Disrupt in San Francisco. The analyst firm HyperFRAME Research, writing on Thursday, says the clearest theme on the expo floor was AI discovery. Companies want to know how their brand shows up when someone asks a chatbot for advice, and startups now sell tools to monitor and improve that. Others, like a startup called Parallel, build search and monitoring tools specifically for AI agents rather than for people. HyperFRAME's conclusion is that the most convincing products targeted one specific task for one clear user.

So why does the LiteLLM story matter for a product manager? Because AI products sit on a thin layer of shared plumbing, and a gateway that holds the keys to every model provider is an attractive target. When your team picks a build or buy option for that layer, ask how updates are pinned and checked, and who can see the keys.

## Politics

Over to politics, and to Belgium, where the federal budget is now officially a November story.

After two late-night meetings of the core cabinet ended without a deal, Prime Minister Bart De Wever told the Chamber on Thursday that a robust proposal is on the table and that the financial markets are watching. He also said he remains confident it will all work out. Talks will continue among the top ministers during next week's parliamentary recess. An attempt by the opposition party Anders to cancel that recess, so that parliament could follow the talks more closely, was voted down.

According to VRT NWS, the new working timeline is a possible overnight deal around the second of November, a policy speech by De Wever on the third, and a parliamentary vote on the full budget before Christmas. Without a deal, Belgium would have an emergency budget for twenty twenty-seven, which VRT's political journalist called an embarrassment. The coalition still wants to find around ten billion euros in savings and new revenue by twenty twenty-nine. The deadline to send the budget to the European Commission, the fifteenth of October, has already passed. VRT also recalls that De Wever had floated a so-called debt brake as a fallback, a simpler emergency rule to avoid provisional twelfths, but most coalition partners rejected it, so the full multi-year budget is back on the table.

The positions are familiar. Vooruit's Frank Vandenbroucke says the current proposals are not fair enough, and his party insists the strongest shoulders must carry their share. CD&V leader Sammy Mahdi called it astonishing that Vooruit had declared a deal unlikely before the meeting even started, but he still expects a result. Les Engagés' deputy prime minister Maxime Prévot says the effort asked of healthcare is still disproportionate, though he accepts that no party will win everything. The MR, for its part, already won its main battle: VRT notes that party leader Georges-Louis Bouchez resisted a VAT increase for weeks and got his way. Prévot also pointed out that Belgium now borrows at higher interest rates than Greece. The opposition, from Groen to Anders, accuses the government of drifting.

Now to the European Parliament, which on Thursday adopted a resolution on Russian attacks in the Black Sea. According to Renew Europe, which proposed the text, recent attacks in Romanian and Bulgarian waters sank three merchant ships and set others on fire, with seafarers killed, injured or missing. The resolution, passed with four hundred and thirty-two votes in favour, holds Russia responsible, calls for tougher sanctions, more counter-drone systems on the Romanian and Bulgarian coasts, and a stronger NATO presence. The Bulgarian news site Sofia Globe reports that the text also criticises the Bulgarian government for what it calls a weak diplomatic response to Moscow, and urges Sofia to stop delaying its participation in a NATO anti-drone initiative.

To the war in Ukraine. Just hours after US envoy Steve Witkoff met Vladimir Putin in Saint Petersburg, Russia launched a large overnight attack on a dozen Ukrainian regions, including Kyiv. According to the news agency AFP, President Zelenskyy said at least two people were killed and thirty-four wounded, including children. Australia's ABC reports a separate strike on a crowded market in the eastern town of Lozova that killed two women. Zelenskyy said the attacks showed "no real change" in Moscow's attitude, and he asked allies for more air defence. Ukraine, for its part, sent hundreds of drones towards Moscow. Russia says it shot down more than eight hundred.

On the diplomacy, the picture is mixed. Witkoff called his talks with Putin constructive, and technical talks between all three sides will continue. The Kremlin says it is willing to restart three-way talks next month, but it dismissed the new ideas Witkoff brought, which were developed with Ukraine and European countries. Moscow also claims a summit between Presidents Trump, Putin and Xi Jinping is being prepared in China for next month. That has not been confirmed by Washington or Beijing. President Trump said on Wednesday that negotiators are maybe the closest they have been to ending the war. AFP reports that in Kyiv and in European capitals, hopes for a breakthrough are slim, and some fear a deal could be struck without them.

## Energy

Over to energy, starting with Europe's winter worries.

The European gas price, measured at the Dutch TTF hub, stood at around ninety-one euros on Thursday per megawatt-hour, according to Trading Economics. That's up more than a quarter in a month and nearly three times the level of a year ago. EU gas storage is just under seventy-five per cent full, the lowest for this time of year since records began.

Against that background, Euronews reports, based on a Council document, that EU governments may ask households and businesses to use less energy. Ireland, which currently chairs the Council of the European Union, put the idea on the table, and EU energy ministers will discuss it next Thursday, the twenty-ninth of October. To be clear, this is a discussion, not a decision. The options range from voluntary appeals to targeted limits for certain sectors, depending on how bad things get. Energy Commissioner Dan Jørgensen has already asked capitals twice to consider demand cuts. Europe's gas grid operators have warned of a possible shortfall of twelve to fifteen per cent if a severe cold snap hits. The background is grim: Euronews notes that the Strait of Hormuz has been effectively closed for almost eight months, and Brent crude traded at around one hundred and ten dollars a barrel on Wednesday. The big open questions are whether cuts should be coordinated at EU level, and how to protect vulnerable households without hurting industry.

Not everyone is suffering. Reuters reports, based on forecasts from the data company LSEG, that Europe's big energy companies are expected to post third-quarter profits about one hundred and thirty-four per cent higher than a year ago, largely thanks to trading desks profiting from the market turmoil around the war with Iran. Excluding energy, the rest of Europe's major listed companies are expected to grow earnings by around eight per cent.

Now to Belgium. Engie has added a feature called Solar Surplus to its Engie Smart app, the Belgian tech site TechPulse reports. Using weather forecasts and your own consumption profile, it predicts, hour by hour for the next three days, when your solar panels will produce more than your home uses. The idea is that you run the washing machine or dryer during those hours. The app doesn't switch anything on itself, and you need solar panels, a digital meter and a suitable contract. Engie points out that savings depend on how much you're paid for power you feed back into the grid. It's a small launch, but it shows how suppliers are trying to shift household demand to the right moment, which matters more and more as grids fill up with solar.

That shift is exactly what Elia is planning for. In its draft federal development plan for twenty twenty-eight to twenty thirty-eight, now open for consultation, Elia says Belgium needs one point eight gigawatts of extra flexibility from consumers and rooftop solar this year, rising to two and a half gigawatts by twenty thirty. Elia is also working on a way to connect large batteries without adding strain to the grid.

And a reminder for the diary: the deadline for the Belgian state and Engie to agree the main terms of a takeover of the nuclear plants is now the fourth of November. According to the news site New Mobility, one of the hardest points is money for dismantling the reactors. The cost is estimated at eleven to twelve billion euros, while Engie has set aside eight point seven billion and intends to stick to that.

## Term of the day

Today's term is a software supply chain attack. Most software today is built from thousands of ready-made pieces written by other people, much like a car factory relies on hundreds of parts suppliers. In a supply chain attack, criminals don't break into your company directly. Instead, they sneak something bad into one of those parts, so it arrives inside a delivery you trust. Imagine a supplier's box of screws where a few are secretly hollow: the factory installs them without a second thought. The usual defence is to lock each part to a known good version, and to check updates before using them. It came up today because the attackers behind the poisoned LiteLLM update did exactly this, slipping their code into an official release.
