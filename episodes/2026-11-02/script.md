## Intro
Good morning Thomas, it's Monday the second of November. Here's your Daily Brief. Today: Belgium's budget talks break down on Sunday night, and Prime Minister Bart De Wever goes to the King. Mistral releases the full weights of its trillion-parameter model. And the United States votes tomorrow in midterm elections that could change who controls Congress.

## AI
We start with AI, and with a European model you can now download and run yourself.

Last Thursday, the French company Mistral released the weights of Mistral Large 4, its biggest model yet. Weights are the trained numbers inside a model. If a company publishes them, anyone can run the model on their own servers, instead of renting it through the maker's cloud. Mistral first showed the model as a public preview on the sixth of October. The company gave it a nickname, "Le Chonk", which tells you something about its size: about one trillion parameters.

That number sounds enormous, and it is. But according to Let's Data Science, citing the consultancy Hexagon Digital, the model uses a design called mixture of experts, and only about forty-nine billion parameters are active for any one task. More on that in today's term of the day. Mistral says the model was trained from scratch on European infrastructure, and that it understands images as well as text. It offers three reasoning modes, low, medium and high, so you can trade speed and cost against depth.

On performance, most of the numbers come from Mistral itself, so treat them with care. Mistral says Large 4 scores forty-eight on the coding agent index of the benchmarking firm Artificial Analysis, roughly level with the Chinese open model GLM five point three, while using about forty-seven per cent fewer tokens per task. A token is a small chunk of text, about three quarters of a word. Mistral also reports strong results in cyber defence. And it's candid about the risk. In its own words, it sees "a potential for uplift to conduct real attacks". During the preview, Hexagon Digital reported that independent tests put the model behind the leading closed systems, but competitive with other open ones.

The licence matters as much as the model. According to a daily AI digest on the developer site DEV Community, the weights come under a modified MIT licence. That's a very permissive open-source licence, but with one catch: companies that want to resell the model as a hosted service need a separate commercial licence from Mistral. Through Mistral's own platform, the listed price starts at seventy cents per million input tokens and two dollars ten per million output tokens.

The contrast with last week is striking. Google released its new frontier model, Gemini 4 Carbon, only to a small group of trusted cyber defenders, because it's so good at finding security holes. Mistral, with a model it also describes as strong in cyber, chose to publish the weights for everyone. Mistral's co-founder and chief scientist Guillaume Lample has framed the company's strategy as working where the big labs don't, saying there are "a lot of areas where the other labs will not focus that much."

Over to the money behind AI, where Friday was a rough day. According to SiliconANGLE, the Nasdaq, the American index full of tech companies, fell almost three per cent on Friday, capping a week of mixed earnings. Alphabet's shares dropped sharply after its results on Wednesday, while Microsoft's rose on forty-eight per cent growth at its Azure cloud. The bigger theme is how these companies pay for their data centres. SiliconANGLE reports that Meta plans to issue up to ninety-five billion dollars in non-voting shares to fund its AI infrastructure, and the DEV Community digest notes reports that Microsoft will raise at least twenty-five billion dollars in bonds for the same reason. So the AI build-out is increasingly financed with debt and new shares, not just with profits. This week, Apple, AMD, Arm and Qualcomm report.

One shorter item. On Thursday, OpenAI published an updated version of its Model Spec. That's the public document describing how its models are supposed to behave. According to the DEV Community digest, the new version is organised around five core values: faithfulness to what the user really intends, minimising harm, honesty and transparency, respecting human agency, and care. OpenAI also dropped much of the detailed guidance on writing style. For anyone building on these models, the spec is worth a read, because it explains why a model refuses or pushes back.

So why does Mistral's release matter for a product manager? Because it widens your build-or-buy choice. If data has to stay in Europe, or on your own servers, a near-frontier model you can host yourself is now a realistic option. But check the licence before you build a product on it, especially if you plan to offer the model to your own customers as a service.

## Politics
Over to politics, and to Belgium first, where the budget talks have hit a wall.

On Sunday evening, Prime Minister Bart De Wever called his core cabinet together at nine o'clock, after a weekend of one-on-one talks with each coalition party. VRT NWS reports that just before eleven, the meeting broke up without an agreement. The French-speaking liberal David Clarinval, of the MR, was first to leave. He blamed Vooruit, the Flemish socialists, for holding out for fewer spending cuts and more taxes. He said more than three billion euros in taxes had been on the table, and told reporters: "I said there were too many taxes." Frank Vandenbroucke of Vooruit and Maxime Prévot of Les Engagés left without comment. According to the news agency Belga, De Wever will see the King today. It isn't yet known what he will tell him.

What is the fight about? The coalition still wants to find about ten billion euros by twenty twenty-nine, to bring the deficit down under European rules. A general rise in VAT, from twenty-one to twenty-two per cent, was dropped after a veto by MR leader Georges-Louis Bouchez. That leaves a hole of roughly three billion euros. According to RTBF, Vooruit wants about one and a half billion euros in extra contributions from the wealthiest, and fewer cuts to health care and pensions. VRT reports that the party also objects to ending sickness benefit after two years for people with burnout or depression, and wants to make management companies less attractive. Those are companies that self-employed people and managers use to pay themselves at a lower tax rate.

Les Engagés, the centrists, said on Sunday that one billion euros from what they call the strongest shoulders is their minimum. RTBF reports they proposed nearly doubling the tax on capital gains, raising the exit tax, and increasing the tax on stock market transactions. An MR source called that list unacceptable and an attack on small businesses. And RTBF reports that De Wever's own party, the N-VA, is unhappy about limits on management companies, which matter to its base of entrepreneurs.

The clock is the real problem. To get a budget through parliament this year, the government needs a deal by about Tuesday. If not, Belgium falls back on so-called provisional twelfths: an emergency regime where the state may spend, each month, one twelfth of last year's budget. De Wever has said he wants to avoid that. Speaking on Radio 1 on Sunday, the Ghent University political scientist Nicolas Bouteca said he was less convinced than a few weeks ago that a deal would come, but added: "Failure is not an option."

Over to the European Union, where young people kept up their protests over the weekend. The Rio Times reports that France deployed around seventy thousand police and gendarmes for a third weekend of demonstrations, with more than forty Paris metro stations closed. In Germany, nearly one hundred and forty thousand people had signed up online for "Gen Z" marches in twenty-three cities, demanding better-funded schools, lower living costs, climate action and an end to compulsory military service. In Italy, protesters clashed with police near the Colosseum during a national strike. The movement started with French school students and has grown into a wider protest about the cost of living and public spending cuts.

Now to the world, and to the United States, where voters go to the polls tomorrow. Republicans hold the Senate fifty-three to forty-seven, and only a razor-thin majority in the House of Representatives. Fox News's latest power rankings call the Senate a toss-up, and point to Democrats retaking the House. Republicans need to hold fifty Senate seats, because Vice President J D Vance breaks ties. Thirty-six states also elect a governor, and voters decide on one hundred and forty-five ballot measures, including a one-time five per cent wealth tax on California billionaires. Fox News describes President Trump's approval as increasingly negative and the war with Iran as unpopular. The first polls close at midnight Belgian time, so expect the picture on Wednesday morning.

And finally, Ukraine. On Friday, the United States approved the possible sale of five hundred Patriot interceptor missiles to Ukraine, worth about two point one billion dollars. CNN reports that America isn't paying: the money comes from Canada, Germany and Norway, plus a European Union loan. These are among the few missiles that can stop Russian ballistic missiles, and Ukraine's supply ran out earlier in October. But no delivery date was given, and President Zelensky warned it could take a few years. He has asked for one hundred interceptors a month from existing stocks this winter.

## Energy
Over to energy, and to Belgium first, where a second deadline lands this week.

On Wednesday, the fourth of November, the government and Engie are supposed to agree the main outline of a deal in which the Belgian state takes over all of Engie's nuclear activities in the country. That date was already a postponement. The original deadline was the first of October, according to a joint statement shared by Belga. As we've reported, De Tijd and L'Echo say draft legislation is ready to nationalise the plants by force if talks fail, though the government hasn't confirmed this. VRT NWS reported last week that the government is considering that option.

Now add the budget crisis. The same small group of ministers who would have to sign off on a nuclear deal spent the weekend locked in budget talks, and Prime Minister De Wever is at the palace today. So watch for one of three things on Wednesday: an outline deal, another postponement, or the first steps towards forced nationalisation. At stake are Doel four and Tihange three, the two reactors still running, which the government wants to keep going for many more years.

Staying with Belgium, but with a European decision. Belgium is one of eleven European Union countries that pledged at an energy ministers' meeting in Luxembourg on Thursday to release diesel from their emergency stocks. According to The Rio Times, they will release twenty and a half million barrels of diesel by the end of January, part of a wider release of more than forty-two million barrels of crude oil and fuels. The other countries include France, Germany, the Netherlands, Luxembourg and Italy. The stocks belong to each country, not to Brussels, and the European Commission will only monitor the release. The reason is supply: conflict in the Middle East has disrupted exports, just as winter demand for diesel from trucks, farms, ships and heating picks up. The Rio Times calls it "a bridge, not a fix", because releasing stocks doesn't add any refining capacity.

Over to Europe's gas market, where a symbolic line was crossed on Friday. According to OilPrice, the Dutch benchmark price, known as TTF, rose to almost one hundred and two euros per megawatt-hour on Friday morning. That's the first time above one hundred euros since twenty twenty-two, and it put the price on track for a weekly gain of about eight per cent.

Two things pushed it up. First, QatarEnergy has extended its force majeure on liquefied gas deliveries until the end of the year. Force majeure is a legal clause that lets a supplier skip deliveries because of events outside its control. Second, there have been several unplanned outages in Norway, Europe's biggest pipeline supplier. Meanwhile, European Union gas storage was just under seventy-five per cent full on Thursday, against a five-year average of about eighty-six per cent for the time of year. And withdrawals have already started in several countries.

There's also a forecast worth noting. Warren Patterson, a commodities strategist at the Dutch bank ING, warned that renewed American strikes on Iran after the midterm elections could make things worse. That's a risk scenario, not a prediction. But it links tomorrow's vote in America directly to what Belgian households and factories may pay for heating this winter.

## Term of the day
Today's term is mixture of experts. It's a way of building a very large AI model so that only a small part of it does the work for each request. Inside the model sit many specialised sub-networks, the experts, plus a small router that decides which few experts should handle each piece of text.

Think of a large hospital. It employs hundreds of doctors, but when you walk in with a broken wrist, you see a triage nurse and one or two specialists, not the whole staff. The hospital has a lot of knowledge, but each visit only uses a little of it.

It came up today because Mistral Large 4 has about one trillion parameters in total, but only around forty-nine billion are active for each task. That's how a giant model can still be reasonably fast and cheap to run.
