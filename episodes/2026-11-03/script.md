## AI
We start with AI, and with a company opening its books.

A quick note first. This podcast is written with Claude, which is made by Anthropic, so I'll stick closely to what the filing and the reporters say.

On Monday, Anthropic posted an updated version of its stock market prospectus with the American Securities and Exchange Commission. A prospectus, or S-1, is the document a company must publish before it sells shares to the public. The first version came out last week. This update adds the results for the third quarter, the three months to the end of September.

According to Fortune, revenue in that quarter was about seventeen billion dollars. That's up from eleven point six billion in the second quarter, and from one point four billion a year earlier. So, more than twelve times bigger than a year ago. But Fortune points out that growth from one quarter to the next slowed to about forty-seven per cent, from about one hundred and forty-four per cent in the quarter before. The filing also says price cuts, linked to new model versions and contract discounts, cost about six hundred million dollars in revenue between the two quarters.

Costs grew faster. Operating expenses rose to seventeen and a half billion dollars, so the company swung from an operating profit of nine hundred and seventy-four million dollars in the second quarter to an operating loss of five hundred and sixty-seven million. The filing blames mainly short-term computing power, which it says costs much more than capacity bought under long multi-year deals. There's also a two-billion-dollar non-cash charge for a programme that matches employee donations. Excluding share-based pay and those charitable charges, Fortune reports the business is still profitable on an adjusted basis, but the adjusted margin fell from nineteen to fifteen per cent.

Then there's the headline loss: twenty-three point three billion dollars for the quarter. That sounds alarming, but according to The Next Web, most of it is an accounting charge on convertible notes, largely held by Amazon. More on that in today's term of the day.

The number that stands out most is the commitments. Anthropic has signed up to about eight hundred and ninety-five billion dollars in future spending, almost all of it on computing power and data centres. The Next Web, citing Bloomberg, says about four hundred and forty-eight billion of that falls due by the end of twenty thirty. Against that, the company reported around one hundred and forty-seven billion dollars in cash, investments and unused credit at the end of September. Anthropic says it plans to move from expensive short-term capacity to cheaper long-term capacity through what it calls Anthropic Direct, its own dedicated infrastructure. The Next Web reports the company plans to list on the Nasdaq exchange, under the ticker A N T, before the end of November. Bloomberg reports investors have discussed a valuation of one point four to one point five trillion dollars. That's talk, not a price.

Over to Apple, and a build-or-partner decision. According to reports relayed by Phemex News, Apple will open its first Apple Intelligence features for testing in China tomorrow, the fourth of November, in the latest developer beta of its iPhone software. In China, those features are paired with Qwen, the model family from the Chinese tech group Alibaba. Chinese users would reach Qwen through Siri, the Writing Tools and Visual Intelligence, with live translation and photo clean-up among the first features. The wider launch is planned for December. Apple's newest Siri features, announced in June, aren't included. The lesson is simple: in a market with its own rules on AI, even Apple chose to work with a local model partner.

So why does Anthropic's filing matter for a product manager? Because it shows the economics under the tools you buy. Model makers are cutting prices to win customers while committing to enormous computing bills. That means prices and usage limits can keep moving, so build your product so you can switch models, and watch your own cost per task closely.

## Politics
Over to politics, and to Belgium first, where the government is in crisis.

On Monday morning, Prime Minister Bart De Wever went to the Royal Palace and offered his resignation to King Philippe. It came after another night of budget talks in the core cabinet ended without a deal. The King didn't accept it. According to Eunews, the Palace said the King "is keeping his decision on hold". In his statement, the King pointed to the lack of agreement on a credible budget, and said the country cannot afford a prolonged political crisis. He urged the parties to put the public interest first and to compromise.

What happens now? From today, the King meets the leaders of the five coalition parties. Eunews reports he will see De Wever again on Friday, the sixth of November. So the coalition has four days to break the deadlock before the King decides whether to accept the resignation. If he does, the government falls. VRT NWS says that wouldn't automatically mean elections: in principle, a new majority could still be found in the current parliament.

The dispute is a classic left-right split, as the VRT political journalist Bart Verhulst puts it. Vooruit, CD&V and Les Engagés want what they call the strongest shoulders, the wealthiest, to contribute more. The N-VA and the MR oppose that. The MR's deputy prime minister David Clarinval objects to "too much taxation". Foreign Minister Maxime Prévot of Les Engagés said listing all the sticking points would take "an eternity". Verhulst's verdict: "If this isn't a political crisis, then I don't know what is."

The stakes are high. The coalition wants to find about ten billion euros by twenty twenty-nine. According to Euronews, Belgium's deficit was five per cent of its economy last year, against a European limit of three, and debt stands above one hundred and ten per cent. Euronews also reports the failed talks pushed up the interest Belgium pays on its bonds. Without a budget voted in time, the state falls back on provisional twelfths, the emergency regime De Wever wants to avoid.

This isn't the first time. In November last year, De Wever also hit a wall on the budget. He didn't resign then, but asked the King for more time, got fifty days, and the coalition agreed on nine point two billion euros in savings. Meanwhile, the unions have called a national strike for the twenty-third of November.

Staying in Brussels, but at European level. According to The National, American negotiators will meet France, Germany, the United Kingdom, the European Union and NATO in Brussels this week. The topic is security guarantees for Ukraine, meaning promises about how Ukraine would be protected after any peace deal.

That's linked to news from the world. On Monday, the American envoy Steve Witkoff said the United States will mediate working-level talks between Ukraine and Russia in Abu Dhabi next week. No exact date was given. He said the talks will cover post-war security, de-escalation and humanitarian issues. The same day, Al Jazeera reports, a Russian strike hit a school used as an aid centre in the village of Komyshuvakha, in the Zaporizhzhia region. Ukraine's emergency services said twenty-four people were killed, and the regional governor said twenty-seven were missing. President Zelensky called it proof that Russia "does not want any kind of de-escalation". Russia denies targeting civilians.

There's also movement on Iran. Reuters, via MarineLink, reports Iranian negotiators have held several days of talks with mediators from Qatar aimed at ending the war, and a draft proposal is circulating. Iran's President Masoud Pezeshkian said Tehran "will try to resolve the problem with dialogue". There's no on-the-record comment from Washington yet.

And finally, the United States, where voters go to the polls today. Yahoo News, in its final round-up of the polls before the weekend, reports Democrats lead the generic ballot, the question of which party you'd vote for in the House, by nearly nine points on average. Democrats need just three seats to win the House. In the Senate, they need to flip four Republican seats, and Yahoo says they are now favoured to win it, adding "unless the polls are wrong". Yahoo also notes that new Republican-drawn district maps across the South could limit Democratic gains. President Trump's approval stands at about thirty-seven per cent in the Silver Bulletin average. The first polls close around midnight Belgian time, so the picture should be clearer tomorrow morning.

## Energy
Over to energy, and to Belgium first, where a deadline lands in the middle of a political crisis.

Tomorrow, the fourth of November, the Belgian state and Engie are supposed to agree the heads of terms for a state takeover of all of Engie's nuclear activities in Belgium. Heads of terms are a short document setting out the main conditions of a deal. They're not yet a binding contract. The deal would cover all seven reactors, the staff, the nuclear subsidiaries and the bill for dismantling the plants, according to Engie's statement from late September. No price has been disclosed.

That deadline was already pushed back once, from the first of October. Last week, VRT NWS reported that both sides might miss it again, and that, according to De Tijd and L'Echo, the government has prepared draft laws to nationalise the plants by force if talks fail. The offices of Energy Minister Mathieu Bihet and Prime Minister De Wever didn't confirm that. Now the Prime Minister's resignation is on the King's desk. So the question for tomorrow is whether a government in this position can sign off on one of the biggest industrial deals in Belgian history, or whether the deadline slips again. We'll know more tomorrow.

Over to Europe's energy markets, where Monday brought some relief. According to Reuters, via MarineLink, oil fell more than seven per cent on Monday on hopes of talks to end the war with Iran. Brent, the international benchmark, dropped to about one hundred and five dollars a barrel. Gas followed. According to Trading Economics, the Dutch gas price, known as TTF, fell sharply on Monday, after topping one hundred euros per megawatt-hour on Friday for the first time since twenty twenty-two. This morning it's trading around ninety-four euros per megawatt-hour.

But analysts warn against reading too much into one day. Pavel Molchanov of Raymond James told Reuters that scepticism is warranted after earlier "false dawns". Giovanni Staunovo of the Swiss bank UBS said the market is reacting with extreme sensitivity to every headline from the Middle East. And Goldman Sachs has a forecast, not a prediction of what will happen, that Brent could reach one hundred and forty dollars in twenty twenty-seven if Gulf output stays well below last year's level. The head of the Abu Dhabi oil company ADNOC, Sultan Al Jaber, said the world has drawn nearly one billion barrels from its stocks since the war disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz.

The underlying problem for Europe is storage. Trading Economics reports the European Union's gas storage was about seventy-four and a half per cent full on the first of November, a historically low level, and Germany's only about sixty-one per cent, below its own target of seventy. Forecasters expect temperatures two to four degrees below normal in major German cities, and withdrawals have already started. So even if prices dip on hopeful headlines, there's less of a cushion for a cold winter.

One more item that links politics and energy. According to The National, President Trump has ordered a sanctions waiver for Russian diesel, citing the global energy crisis. That comes only weeks after he signed new sanctions raising duties on Russian goods by up to five hundred per cent. It shows how high fuel prices are now shaping sanctions policy.

## Term of the day
Today's term is convertible note. It's a loan to a company that can later turn into shares instead of being paid back in cash. Investors like it because, if the company grows, their loan becomes a slice of something much more valuable.

Think of lending a friend money to open a bakery, on the condition that, if the bakery takes off, you can swap the loan for part-ownership. The better the bakery does, the more your claim is worth.

It came up today because of Anthropic's twenty-three-billion-dollar quarterly loss. Under accounting rules, those notes are revalued every quarter. As the company's value goes up, the notes are worth more to the investors who hold them, and that increase is recorded as a loss for the company, even though no cash leaves the bank. The notes are expected to turn into shares at the stock market listing.
