## Intro
Good morning Thomas, it's Thursday the nineteenth of November. Today: Google launches Gemini 4 Neon, a model built to sit between cheap and premium. Anthropic sets a record price for its stock market listing. In Europe, the Commission proposes a new forty-five billion euro loan for Ukraine. And in energy, Belgium's nuclear deadline with Engie is now just over three weeks away.

## AI
We start with AI, and with a model launch that is really a pricing decision dressed up as a technology story.

On Wednesday, Google released Gemini 4 Neon. According to Google's own announcement, written by Koray Kavukcuoglu of Google DeepMind, Neon is aimed at complex coding, analysis, and business and scientific work. It sits between two models Google already sells. Carbon is the heavyweight, for the hardest and longest projects. Hydrogen is the fast, cheap one for everyday tasks. Neon is meant to balance the two.

The price is three dollars per million tokens going in, and fifteen dollars per million coming out. A token is roughly three quarters of a word. If you send the same material again, for example a long set of instructions at the start of every request, that repeated input costs only fifteen cents per million. Like its siblings, Neon can take in about a million tokens at once.

It also has four so-called thinking levels, which let you choose how hard the model works on a problem before it answers. We'll come back to that in the term of the day. Google says that at its default level, Neon costs thirty-two per cent less per task than Carbon.

On benchmarks, Google says Neon ranks first on the coding agent index run by the independent firm Artificial Analysis, and third on its general intelligence index. VentureBeat did a careful comparison and found a more nuanced picture. Neon is a quarter cheaper per token than Anthropic's top model, Claude Opus five point five, but half as expensive again as Claude Sonnet and OpenAI's Sol, which both cost two dollars in and ten out. On some coding tests, Opus still leads. And VentureBeat points out that many of the headline numbers were chosen or supplied by Google itself. Its arithmetic suggests Neon would need about a third fewer tokens than Sol or Sonnet to finish a job before it actually works out cheaper.

Neon is available now for paying developers through Google's AI Studio, for businesses through Gemini for business, and in the Gemini app and Google's coding tool Antigravity for paying subscribers.

Staying with models, Microsoft used its Ignite conference this week to show it is serious about building its own, rather than relying only on OpenAI and Anthropic. According to The Daily Star, Microsoft launched two coding models, MAI Code 2 and a lighter version called MAI Code 2 Flash. Microsoft claims the main model reaches ninety-six per cent of Claude Opus's performance on four coding tests, at just five per cent of the cost. Those are Microsoft's own figures, and Microsoft admits the models are not yet tuned for very long, autonomous tasks or hard scientific problems. Both are available now inside GitHub Copilot. And Microsoft says another in-house model, MAI Work Flash, already powers features in Microsoft three sixty-five, where it claims it matches or beats OpenAI's models on its own internal tests for Excel, PowerPoint, Word and Teams. That's a clear build-versus-buy choice. For everyday coding help in its own products, Microsoft has decided it is cheaper to build than to keep paying partners.

And finally, Anthropic, the company that makes Claude, which also writes this podcast. As we previewed yesterday, it set the price for its stock market listing last night. According to TechCrunch, the shares were priced at eighty-three dollars each, above the range of seventy to seventy-eight dollars on offer. That values the company at about one and a half trillion dollars on a fully diluted basis, meaning counting all shares and options. Yahoo Finance reports that orders came in at nearly seven times the number of shares available. CNBC reports the listing raises more than eighty-one billion dollars, which it says would make it the largest offering on record, ahead of SpaceX. Trading on the Nasdaq exchange, under the ticker A N T, starts today. Yahoo Finance also offers a word of caution: every one of the twelve largest American listings since two thousand and eight fell below its first trading price within a year.

So why does the Neon launch matter for a product manager? Because the middle of the market is now crowded, and price per token is no longer the whole story. What matters is the cost to finish a real task in your product, so measure that, on your own work, before you switch.

## Politics
Over to politics, and to Belgium first, where the Prime Minister is now talking to everyone except the party that said no.

According to VRT News, citing Het Nieuwsblad, Bart De Wever held separate meetings on Tuesday and Wednesday with the vice-prime ministers of CD&V, MR and Les Engagés. He did not meet Vooruit's Frank Vandenbroucke. As we've covered, Vooruit was the only party to reject De Wever's budget proposal at the weekend.

Vooruit isn't backing down. On Wednesday evening, the party repeated on social media that it wants large fortunes to contribute. Its message contrasted smaller index rises for pensions and the loss of a day off with what it called a refusal to look at big wealth. But VRT reports that the idea of taking away a day of leave is no longer on the table, and one coalition party says the information about adjusting the index is wrong. In other words, each side is disputing what is actually in the proposal. The VRT journalist Pieterjan De Smedt described it as a brutal power struggle, with the parties holding each other in a stranglehold.

The timetable hasn't changed. De Wever is due back at the palace on Monday, when the trade unions are also holding a demonstration in Brussels.

Away from the budget, two other stories from the federal parliament. The defence minister, Theo Francken, told the Chamber that his team and the interior minister, Bernard Quintin, broadly agree in principle on a military-trained police unit for large riots, nicknamed "Rijkswacht two point oh", the old name for the gendarmerie. Francken says defence could contribute about four hundred people. And the justice minister, Annelies Verlinden, told a committee she can keep doing her job, even though the prosecutor-general is examining her role in the troubled i-Police computer project.

Over to the European Union, where Ukraine's money for next year is now on the table. On Wednesday, Commission President Ursula von der Leyen proposed a new loan of forty-five billion euros for Ukraine in twenty twenty-seven. According to Euronews, twenty-five billion would go to the military and twenty billion to budget support. Radio Free Europe reports it would cover about two thirds of Ukraine's expected seventy billion euro gap, with Brussels hoping countries like Canada, Japan, Norway and Britain cover the rest. Ukraine would only repay once Russia pays war reparations. Von der Leyen said this would give Kyiv a strong hand at the negotiating table.

But it's a proposal, not a decision, and it needs all twenty-seven countries to agree. Euronews reports that Hungary, Slovakia, Czechia and Bulgaria are resisting. Germany's Chancellor Friedrich Merz has threatened to hold up talks on the EU's next long-term budget unless every member contributes. And there's a Belgian angle: according to Radio Free Europe, Belgium is still blocking the use of frozen Russian assets held at Euroclear in Brussels as collateral, with diplomats saying France and Luxembourg quietly back that position. EU leaders discuss the plan at a summit next week.

Today, Merz hosts more than twenty leaders of the so-called Coalition of the Willing in Berlin. According to Ukrinform, he set two goals: paying for Ukraine's defence in twenty twenty-seven, and a common European position ahead of any peace talks with Russia.

And to the wider world, where the same day brings a meeting without precedent. Donald Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin are due to hold three-way talks in Shenzhen today, on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific summit China is hosting. Euronews reports that Trump and Putin shook hands on Wednesday, their first meeting since twenty twenty-five. At a business event on the sidelines, Putin told company bosses that sanctions, tariffs and export restrictions had, in his words, outlived their usefulness. That's his position; Europe and America still have those sanctions in place. According to Al Jazeera, the White House says Trump will discuss Iran and a possible three-way nuclear arrangement. Ukraine is not expected to be formally on the agenda, and analysts expect few concrete results. One, Vuk Vuksanovic of King's College London, said it was far from being a new Yalta, the nineteen forty-five conference that divided post-war Europe. On trade, the truce between America and China has been extended to January, but China's exports of rare earth metals to the United States remain far below normal.

## Energy
Over to energy, and to Belgium first, where the nuclear deadline is creeping closer while the government is busy elsewhere.

There's no new announcement this week, but the clock is worth noting. According to World Nuclear News, the Belgian state and Engie have until the twelfth of December to agree the main terms for the state to take over Engie's entire nuclear business in Belgium. That covers all seven reactors, the staff, and the obligation to dismantle the plants one day. The original target was the first of October, then the fourth of November. Both sides report progress, but say significant differences remain, especially over the price. Ad-hoc News, citing The Brussels Times, adds that the long-term costs of nuclear waste and dismantling are the other big sticking point.

The government has also prepared a fallback. In late October, EU Alive reported that De Wever had ordered draft legislation that would allow the state to expropriate the plants, setting the compensation itself. That's described as leverage rather than a plan, and it would almost certainly lead to years in court.

Why does this matter? Because Belgium has become unusually dependent on its neighbours. As we reported yesterday, The Brussels Times found that Belgium's power production in September was the lowest since records began in two thousand and eight, and the country imported a record amount of electricity in the first half of the year.

Over to Europe, where gas is still the number to watch. As we covered yesterday, prices have been climbing ahead of a cold spell. The underlying picture hasn't changed. According to OilPrice, EU gas storage is about seventy per cent full, compared with eighty-nine per cent at this point last year. Energy Flux reports that Europe has been drawing gas from storage at nearly three times the normal pace, partly because of a spell of cold, windless weather in Germany. Norway, Europe's biggest supplier, has unplanned outages at two of its fields. Liquefied gas through the Strait of Hormuz is running at roughly half of pre-war levels, and QatarEnergy has extended its force majeure, a legal notice that it can't meet deliveries, until at least the end of December. On top of that, the EU's ban on Russian liquefied gas under long-term contracts starts on the first of January. Energy Flux's author warns that storage could end the winter critically low. That's one analyst's forecast, not a certainty, and it depends heavily on the weather.

And finally, the climate summit, COP31 in Antalya, which is due to end tomorrow. As Reuters reported, the draft deal offers two versions on fossil fuels: one repeats the twenty twenty-three wording on transitioning away from them, the other calls for a phase-out. Either could be dropped. The draft also flags disagreement over Turkey's proposal to raise electricity's share of the world's energy use to thirty-five per cent by twenty thirty-five. According to Euronews, the head of the International Energy Agency, Fatih Birol, backed that same thirty-five per cent target this week. For Europe, electrification is also an energy security argument: the more cars and heating run on home-grown electricity, the less exposed the continent is to the gas shocks we've just described. Expect the talks to run late, as they usually do.

## Term of the day
Today's term is thinking levels, sometimes called reasoning effort.

Many new AI models can think before they answer. That means working through a problem in hidden steps, checking and rethinking, before writing a reply. Thinking levels let you choose how much of that the model does. More thinking usually means better answers on hard problems, but it takes longer and costs more, because you pay for every token the model uses while thinking.

Think of asking a colleague for help. For a quick question, you want an answer in the corridor. For a tricky contract, you want them to take the afternoon.

It came up today because Google's Gemini 4 Neon has four thinking levels, and VentureBeat warns that "maximum" means very different things at different companies. For a product team, this is a setting you tune per feature, not once for the whole product.
