## Intro
Good morning Thomas, it's Tuesday the twenty-seventh of October. Here's your Daily Brief. Today: Anthropic files to go public, and its paperwork shows how an AI company grows inside big customers. Israel votes in a closely fought election, while Belgium's budget talks resume tomorrow. And European gas reaches its highest price in four years, even as oil drops on hopes of diplomacy with Iran.

## AI
We start with AI, and with a filing that tells us more about how a frontier AI business really works than any keynote could.

On Monday, Anthropic, the company behind the Claude models, officially filed to list its shares on the stock market. TechCrunch reports that it plans to trade under the ticker A N T. It has not yet said how much money it wants to raise or at what share price. A quick note on transparency: Anthropic also makes the model that writes this podcast, so treat this as straight reporting from the filing and from TechCrunch.

So what's in the numbers? According to the filing, revenue for the first half of this year was sixteen point three billion dollars, up from one point two billion in the same period last year. The operating loss narrowed to six hundred and ninety-three million dollars, from two point one billion. The headline net loss is enormous, more than eighty billion dollars, but TechCrunch explains that this is mostly an accounting effect linked to convertible debt, not cash going out the door. The company still warns that it may never become profitable.

The governance is unusual. The seven co-founders will hold just over half of shareholder voting power through a special entity, and a separate trust, set up to protect the company's public benefit mission, can appoint most of the board. In plain terms, public shareholders will own a piece of the company but will have little say over how it's run. TechCrunch calls that controversial. The prospectus also repeats the company's long-standing warning that its technology could pose catastrophic risks.

For a product manager, the most interesting part is a second TechCrunch piece on how Anthropic grows inside its customers. More than half of customers who spend at least a hundred thousand dollars a year now use both the developer platform and one of Anthropic's own apps, such as Claude Code or Cowork. At the end of last year that share was about thirty per cent. The typical path, according to the filing, starts with developers using the raw model through an interface for programmers, then moves to Claude Code for engineers, and then to Cowork for the wider workforce. TechCrunch reports net revenue retention of around nine hundred per cent at the end of the third quarter. That's today's term of the day, so hold that thought.

There's also a lesson about distribution. Anthropic sells through Amazon, Google and Microsoft's cloud marketplaces, but it keeps the direct contract with the customer and pays the cloud provider a fee. The filing says this helps it avoid channel conflict and keep control of pricing. It also admits a risk: those same cloud giants supply its computing power, and they may favour their own models or rivals they've invested in.

Staying with the money behind AI. Meta wants to raise up to ninety-five billion dollars in new equity to pay for its AI build-out. According to Reuters, as carried by The Spokesman-Review, the plan combines about thirty-five billion dollars in public offerings of common and preferred stock with a separate programme of up to sixty billion dollars that sells shares gradually on the market. Reuters calls it Meta's largest-ever equity raise. Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom, the chip makers that sell Meta its hardware, have signalled interest in buying up to five billion dollars of the shares. Meta reported negative free cash flow in the third quarter, its first in more than a decade, and recently raised the bottom of its annual spending forecast to one hundred and thirty-five billion dollars. The shares fell modestly on the news.

And finally, a claim that will need careful checking. PANews, citing an announcement by OpenAI, reports that the company's internal models have produced three major results in mathematics and physics, including a claimed proof that a widely used assumption in computer science, the Exponential Time Hypothesis, is false. Hundreds of research papers rely on that assumption. OpenAI says the proof has been checked by a computer proof assistant and will be reviewed by the researchers who first proposed the hypothesis. For now, these are claims. Independent experts have not yet confirmed them.

Why does the Anthropic filing matter to you as a product manager? Because it shows the land-and-expand playbook in hard numbers: win developers with a platform, then sell finished apps to more and more people in the same company. If you're building an AI product, think about which layer you own, and what your customer's second and third purchase from you could be.

## Politics
Over to politics, and to Belgium first.

The federal budget is still stuck, and the top ministers are due back at the table tomorrow. Deputy Prime Minister Jan Jambon told VRT NWS that technical staff are working through Monday and Tuesday, and that ministers will meet again in earnest from Wednesday. VRT describes the coalition as deadlocked after Vooruit, the Flemish socialists, put the brakes on Prime Minister Bart De Wever's all-or-nothing push last week. CD and V chair Sammy Mahdi said over the weekend he was disappointed by that, and stressed that the end-of-year deadline cannot move.

The clock is now the real problem. Business AM reports that De Wever wants to put an agreement to parliament by the third of November, followed by a confidence vote. Opposition MP Vincent Van Quickenborne of Anders argues that is too late, because budget documents usually take about a month to process. If the budget is not voted by the first of January, Belgium falls back on what are called provisional twelfths. That means each month the government may only spend one twelfth of the previous year's budget, and it cannot start new policies or new taxes. Business AM adds that the parliament's chair may even consider cancelling the Christmas recess.

What's actually on the table? According to The Brussels Times, which cites a seventy-eight-page working document, the proposals include about four billion euros of savings in health care, such as raising the patient's contribution for a GP visit. A general rise in VAT has been dropped, but targeted increases are proposed, for example on hotel stays and firewood. There's also a target of one billion euros from higher taxes on the wealthiest, partly through management companies. None of this is final. MR leader Georges-Louis Bouchez says everyday shopping must not become more expensive, while Vooruit says the package is not yet fair.

Now to the European Union. The European Commission meets today, and according to its indicative planning, reported by Eunews, the agenda includes a climate resilience framework and a border and migration package that would give more powers to Frontex, the EU's border agency. Next week, on the fourth of November, the Commission is due to present its annual enlargement package, covering countries such as Ukraine, Moldova, Albania and Montenegro. EU leaders also meet Canada's government for a summit later this week, according to the Council's calendar.

Over to the world, and to Israel, where voters go to the polls today. Polls are open until ten in the evening local time. According to the Turkish news agency Anadolu, the centrist party Yashar, led by former army chief Gadi Eisenkot, edged ahead of Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's Likud in the final surveys. Al Jazeera reports that a poll by the public broadcaster Kan gave the opposition parties fifty-two seats and Netanyahu's bloc fifty. A majority needs sixty-one of the hundred and twenty seats, so neither bloc looks set to win outright. That could give Arab-led parties a key role, or lead to long coalition talks. The campaign has centred on accountability for the October twenty twenty-three attacks, the war, and military service exemptions for ultra-Orthodox men. Official results are due by the fourth of November.

In Brazil, President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva has won a record fourth term. According to Latin Times, citing the electoral court's final count, Lula took fifty-one point seven six per cent against Flávio Bolsonaro's forty-eight point two four. It was a comeback: Lula had finished second in the first round. In his victory speech, Lula said the country needs peace and unity. CNN reports that Bolsonaro has rejected the result. Lula is due to be sworn in on the first of January.

And on Russia and Ukraine, Euronews reports that President Volodymyr Zelenskyy has confirmed new three-way talks with Russia and the United States in November. According to Kremlin adviser Yuri Ushakov, Vladimir Putin agreed in principle to restart talks after the tenth of November. Ushakov said Moscow wants the talks to focus on a security system for Russia and Ukraine once the fighting ends. Zelenskyy said territorial questions must be settled at leaders' level. Earlier rounds this year, in Abu Dhabi and Geneva, produced little beyond prisoner exchanges, and both sides still hold their core demands.

## Energy
Over to energy, and to the price that matters most for Belgian heating bills this winter.

On Monday morning, the Dutch benchmark price for European gas, known as TTF, rose to almost ninety-six euros per megawatt-hour. According to Mansfield Energy, that's the highest level since twenty twenty-two, after a seven per cent rise the week before. Traders are worried because the winter season of drawing gas out of storage has started, and there's no sign that liquefied gas shipments from Qatar will return soon. The backdrop is thin stocks. According to Agence Europe, EU gas storage was just over seventy-two per cent full in early October, the lowest level for that time of year since records began in twenty eleven. The European Commission has already agreed to lower the filling target for the first of December from ninety to eighty per cent.

Oil moved the other way. According to Euronews, the international benchmark, Brent, fell more than six per cent on Monday, from about one hundred and seventeen dollars a barrel at Friday's close to around one hundred and ten. The reason is diplomacy. Qatari mediators reportedly travelled to Tehran on Sunday, and senior Saudi, Pakistani and Turkish officials were due there on Monday. President Donald Trump told Fox News he is weighing whether to make a deal with Iran. At the same time, Houthi rebels in Yemen say they struck Saudi energy sites again over the weekend. So any calm in the market is fragile.

Staying with Belgium. The countdown is on for the government's talks with Engie about taking over the country's nuclear power stations. The deadline was already pushed back once, from the first of October to the fourth of November, in a joint statement by the Prime Minister, the Energy Ministry and Engie, according to newmobility news, citing Belgian media. The government wants the Doel four and Tihange three reactors to run until twenty forty-five. The tough questions are money. Dismantling the plants could cost eleven to twelve billion euros, while Engie has set aside eight point seven billion and wants to stick to that. Advisers from Rothschild and KPMG are checking whether a longer life is affordable and legally possible. Remember that this lands in the same week as the budget deadline, so any nuclear deal will also be judged on what it means for public finances.

Over to Europe. EU energy ministers meet in Luxembourg on Thursday. According to the Council's forward look, they'll discuss a proposed regulation on electricity network charges, which are the fees on your bill that pay for the grid, as well as energy security and possible targets for after twenty thirty. Agence Europe reports that several countries are pushing back on the network charges proposal. Finland says it relies on too much harmonisation. Luxembourg, Austria, Poland and France question giving the Commission power to set common methods. And Hungary, France and Portugal oppose naming specific groups, such as energy-intensive industry or data centres, for cheaper tariffs. In a separate briefing note, Agence Europe reports that the Irish Council presidency wants ministers to discuss how to prepare if the Strait of Hormuz stays partly or fully closed, and whether EU-wide measures to cut demand should be considered. That second question is a debate, not yet a proposal.

## Term of the day
Today's term is net revenue retention. It measures how much more, or less, a group of existing customers pays you this year compared with last year, ignoring any new customers you've won. If a hundred customers paid you one hundred thousand euros last year, and the same customers pay one hundred and twenty thousand this year, your net revenue retention is one hundred and twenty per cent. Think of a gym that keeps its members and sells them personal training on top. It came up today because Anthropic's stock market filing reports a figure of around nine hundred per cent. That means the average customer spent many times more than a year earlier, which is the land-and-expand story in a single number.
