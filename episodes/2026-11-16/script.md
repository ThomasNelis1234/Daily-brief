## Intro

## AI
We start with AI, and with a model that's less glamorous than a frontier launch, but probably more important for the people who pay the bills.

On Thursday, Google released Gemini 4 Hydrogen. According to Google's developer blog, it's built for everyday coding and agentic workflows, meaning tasks where the model works through several steps on its own, like fixing a bug across a code base or building a small web app. It's available through the Gemini programming interface in Google AI Studio and Android Studio, in Gemini for business, and in Google's coding tool Antigravity for paying subscribers. Google says it follows instructions more tightly and makes fewer fabricated claims. It can also work alongside the bigger models, Gemini 4 Carbon and Gemini 4 Argon. In Antigravity, a feature called Orchestrator pairs Hydrogen with Carbon, so the cheaper model does the routine work and the expensive one is called in when needed.

The interesting part is the price. VentureBeat has gone through the numbers. Hydrogen replaces Gemini 3.8 Flash, Google's budget model. It costs one dollar fifty per million tokens of input and seven dollars fifty per million tokens of output. A token is a small chunk of text, roughly three quarters of a word. That's double what Flash costs today. But Flash itself is scheduled to rise to exactly the same price on the first of January. So in effect, the cheap tier is getting more expensive, and Google is offering a better model to soften the blow.

And it does seem better. The independent benchmarking firm Artificial Analysis gives Hydrogen a score of fifty-one on its intelligence index, against thirty-one for Flash. Google says it finishes tasks about two and a half times as fast as Flash at its default setting. The storage company Box tested it early, and according to VentureBeat, its accuracy on a complex work test rose from seventy-four to seventy-nine per cent, with tasks taking less than half as long.

There are catches for developers. The default reasoning level is now high, so out of the box the model thinks harder, and you pay for those thinking tokens. Hydrogen also rejects an older setting called the thinking budget, so some existing code will need changes. And VentureBeat notes that Hydrogen's price per token is roughly fifteen times the lowest standard rates of small rival models from Anthropic, OpenAI and others. The way to keep costs down is to choose a lower reasoning level for simple tasks, reuse cached input, and send work that isn't urgent through cheaper batch processing.

Staying with AI products, and with a disclosure first: this podcast is written with Claude, which is made by Anthropic, so I'll stick closely to what the sources say. Anthropic has opened a beta of Claude Meetings on Google Meet. According to Dataconomy, paying Team and Enterprise customers can invite Claude into a video call as a visible participant. People can ask it things out loud or in the chat, and it can draft a plan, look things up in connected tools with permission, or sketch a schedule on a shared whiteboard. Afterwards it creates a page with notes, action items and a transcript. It doesn't keep audio. By default, it stays out of calls with outside guests and leaves if one joins. Anthropic also made interactive connectors generally available, which let apps from Salesforce, Atlassian, HubSpot and others open inside Claude. Google Meet is the first platform, and Anthropic says others will follow.

Finally, the fallout from Nvidia continues, and it's turning into a policy story. Startup Fortune reports that after Jensen Huang's resignation last week, the Speaker of the House, Mike Johnson, spoke of a growing consensus in Congress on limiting chip exports to China. Republicans want to attach new restrictions to the annual defence bill. One proposal would stop Chinese firms from renting computing power in overseas data centres that use Nvidia hardware. None of these bills has passed yet, so these are proposals, not rules. Several committee chairs want Huang to testify, but the reported dates don't line up.

So why does Hydrogen matter for a product manager? Because the era of nearly free small models is ending, at least at Google. If your product calls a budget model millions of times, check your cost model now, before January, and treat the reasoning level as a product decision, not just a technical default.

## Politics
Over to politics, and to Belgium first, where this is the morning the whole country has been counting down to.

At half past eleven, Prime Minister Bart De Wever reports to King Philippe at the palace. It was the King who set this date, when he refused De Wever's resignation earlier this month and asked him to resume talks immediately. De Wever goes there without a full agreement.

Here's how the weekend went, according to VRT News. On Friday evening, the full core cabinet met for the first time since the first of November. On Saturday, working groups took over, and on Sunday at two o'clock the core cabinet met again for what was billed as a final round. VRT journalist Yves Borms reported that nine of the ten billion euros had been found, and that the last billion was the hardest. By half past eight on Sunday evening, the meeting ended without a deal.

The other parties pointed in one direction. David Clarinval of the MR said his party was not the problem, and that four parties agreed while only Vooruit did not. Maxime Prévot of Les Engagés said Vooruit's refusal was harming the process, and complained that leaks of measures on the table were undermining trust. Jan Jambon of the N-VA said earlier in the day that all five parties had a responsibility to reach an agreement.

Vooruit sees it differently. Deputy Prime Minister Frank Vandenbroucke said his party had made compromises, but that the others had not offered enough on contributions from what he called the strongest shoulders. He wants a small but robust contribution from people with very large fortunes. Party president Conner Rousseau said he was enormously disappointed, and wants less harsh measures on healthcare, pensions and the indexation of wages. He said Vooruit unfortunately stands alone in the government, and accepted that others would blame his party.

So what happens today? Borms expects De Wever to go to the palace without a full deal. The other parties hope he can present a broad framework, so talks can continue in the coming days. Borms also suggested the King might take steps to help the talks along. If there's no budget by the new year, VRT reported earlier that Belgium would likely fall back on an emergency budget in monthly instalments, based on twenty twenty-six.

Staying with Belgium, the social partners also weighed in on Sunday. Pieter Timmermans, head of the employers' federation VBO, called the government's earlier choice on wage indexation the biggest disappointment of his career, and asked for an overall approach rather than small measures. Ann Vermorgen of the Christian union ACV opposed any plan to scrap a public holiday, saying ordinary workers were being asked to give up more.

That brings us to the European Union, and to Dublin, where EU leaders met informally on Friday. According to EU Alive, they agreed who will represent Europe in peace talks on Ukraine: the European Commission and the Council, plus five countries from the so-called Coalition of the Willing, which are France, Germany, Italy, Poland and the United Kingdom. Xinhua, citing EUobserver, notes that this mostly formalises an existing arrangement, and that a single EU envoy is still undecided. Analysts quoted by EU Alive see it as a setback for the EU's foreign policy chief, Kaja Kallas.

The summit was also the leaders' first in-depth discussion on artificial intelligence, with Arthur Mensch of Mistral and the Nobel laureate Geoffrey Hinton as guests. Ursula von der Leyen, the President of the Commission, said Europe needs at least five hundred billion euros for technological sovereignty. There was no new binding money. Le Monde, as cited by Xinhua, reports that the plan for AI gigafactories, very large computing centres, is stalled and tied to talks on the next long-term EU budget. Leaders return to Ukraine's financing at their summit on the twenty-sixth and twenty-seventh of November, and to AI in February.

Which brings us to Ukraine itself. According to the Australian broadcaster ABC, President Zelenskyy said American negotiators asked whether Ukraine would withdraw from the Donbas region if it could join NATO. He said it was the first time NATO membership had been linked to giving up territory in these talks. Zelenskyy said Kyiv is still not ready to give up land, but would put a deal to a national referendum if it came with security guarantees, a strong and well-funded army, and money for reconstruction. Russia has long called Ukrainian NATO membership a red line, and ABC reports no official Russian response to the idea. On the prisoner exchange we mentioned on Friday, ABC says the swap would involve more than a thousand people, with final numbers still being negotiated. No date has been set for the next round of talks.

## Energy
Over to energy, and to Belgium first, where the budget crisis is holding up a deal that matters for the next decade.

As we've followed, the federal government and Engie are negotiating a state takeover of all of Engie's nuclear activities in Belgium. After two missed deadlines, the new target is the twelfth of December, for an agreement in principle. According to The Brussels Times, a deal needs direct talks between Bart De Wever and Engie's chief executive, Catherine MacGregor. But the Prime Minister's attention is fully on the budget. The same paper notes that Elia's investment plan for the high-voltage grid from twenty twenty-eight to twenty thirty-eight partly depends on what happens to the reactors. So whatever the King hears this morning, it also affects the nuclear file. The longer the budget crisis runs, the less time remains before that December date.

And it comes as bills hurt. According to the latest Eurostat figures, published at the end of October and summarised by EU Perspectives, Belgian households paid about thirty-six cents per kilowatt-hour for electricity in the first half of this year. That's level with Germany for the second-highest price in the Union, behind only Ireland. The EU average was about twenty-eight cents. Gas gave more relief across Europe, with average household prices down about ten per cent.

Over to the European gas market, where the price kept climbing at the end of last week. According to IndexBox, drawing on Trading Economics, the Dutch benchmark gas price, known as TTF, rose above ninety-four euros per megawatt-hour on Friday, a two-week high. On Wednesday, as we reported, it was around eighty-seven. Three things are pushing it up. First, the Middle East, where President Trump said Washington has no need for a deal with Iran, and tanker attacks continue around the Strait of Hormuz. Second, Norway, where deliveries fell partly because of unplanned outages at the Troll and Asgard gas facilities. And third, the weather. Forecasts point to colder conditions in parts of Europe from tomorrow, with possible further cold spells into early December. That matters because EU storage was under seventy-two per cent full, against a five-year average of about eighty-nine. Bloomberg, as published by Energy Connects, reported that a German storage group warned supply cannot be fully guaranteed this winter if it gets extremely cold. That's a warning, not a forecast, and most weather models still point to a fairly mild winter.

Finally, the climate summit COP31 in Antalya enters its final week. According to Earth dot org, the United Nations circulated a summary of countries' views on Friday, and a first draft of the final deal is expected as early as today. The Australian president of negotiations, Chris Bowen, said nothing in it is settled. One idea in the mix is a pledge for electricity to supply thirty-five per cent of the world's final energy by twenty thirty-five. Carbon Brief understands that more than fifty countries back it. But Carbon Brief's own analysis also finds that public climate finance pledged by major developed countries, such as Germany, France and Japan, is about seventy per cent lower than in twenty twenty-five. The European Commission's climate finance lead said budgets are difficult everywhere, including in the EU.

## Term of the day
Today's term is reasoning level, sometimes called thinking level. Many new AI models think before they answer. They write out hidden working steps, like a student doing sums on scrap paper before giving the result. The reasoning level is a dial that sets how much scrap paper the model may use. Low means quick answers, high means slower but more careful ones. Think of a taxi driver: for a short trip you just drive, but for a complicated route across town you check the map first. The catch is that every one of those hidden steps is billed as output tokens. It came up today because Google's Gemini 4 Hydrogen now defaults to the high setting. VentureBeat's figures show the cost per task more than doubles from medium to high, so picking the right level for each task is one of the easiest ways to control your AI bill.
