## AI
We start with AI, and with a launch that is aimed squarely at people who look after products, rather than people who build them from scratch.

On Wednesday evening, our time, Google Labs announced Stitch Loop. Stitch is Google's AI design tool, which turns plain text into user interfaces. Loop takes the idea a step further. According to coverage by Superpower Daily and the agency blog Digital Applied, it is an always-on agent for software that already has users. You connect it to your code on GitHub, and you feed it evidence: logs, user behaviour, feedback and product documents. Google describes more than a hundred and eighty integrations, plus support for custom connectors through the Model Context Protocol, the open standard we've discussed before.

Then you give it a standing goal. Google's own example is improving checkout conversion. The agent looks for evidence, suggests fixes with their trade-offs, and once a person picks a direction, it writes the code in an isolated cloud sandbox and opens a pull request, which is a proposed change that a developer reviews before it goes in. After release, it watches the live numbers and uses them to shape its next suggestions. It can even keep watching after a target is reached, to catch a later decline.

Google ships three ready-made loops: speed, accessibility and security. For security, Google says its top model, Gemini 4 Carbon, works with a DeepMind tool called CodeMender to find weaknesses and propose patches. You can also use it from Slack, with daily digests of what it has noticed.

Two important limits. First, every change is an ordinary pull request that a human has to approve. Superpower Daily stresses that this is not autonomous deployment. Second, it's experimental. There's a global waitlist, priority for subscribers to Google's top AI Ultra plan, and no price or launch date yet. Digital Applied also notes that Google has made no technical commitments yet on how the sandbox is isolated or what data it keeps.

Next, Anthropic, the company that makes Claude, which also writes this podcast. As promised yesterday, here's how its first day of trading went. According to the live tracker of the financial site Stock Titan, the shares didn't trade at the opening bell. The first trade came at eleven forty-eight in the morning, New York time, at a hundred and eleven dollars fifty, about thirty-four per cent above the offer price of eighty-three dollars. They briefly touched almost a hundred and fifteen dollars, then drifted down, and closed at ninety-seven dollars seventeen. That's a gain of seventeen per cent on the offer price, but almost thirteen per cent below the first trade. Around eight hundred and thirty million shares changed hands. So, a solid debut rather than a frenzy. Investors were willing to pay more than the banks had set, but not much more than that after the first excited minutes. Stock Titan also reports that a separate sale of twenty-six billion dollars of non-voting shares to seven technology companies is due to close today.

Now a story about who carries the risk when an AI agent handles your money. According to AI Weekly, summarising a TechCrunch report, three American banks, Citi, PNC and Citizens, updated their terms on Wednesday. Customers who let third-party AI agents into their accounts are now responsible for whatever those agents do, even if the customer's login is leaked or the agent itself is hacked. Citi makes an exception for agents it provides itself. The trade publication The Paypers reported last month that JPMorgan and Bank of America had already moved to restrict outside agents, with Bank of America saying a human should approve money leaving an account. So five of the biggest American banks are now pushing that risk onto customers, at least until the rules on liability are settled.

And briefly, chips. The Neuron newsletter, citing The Verge, reports that Nvidia has stopped shipping advanced AI chips to China, even though Washington had approved some sales. Nvidia's interim chief executive, Colette Kress, said no further licensed shipments of its older Hopper chips were planned, after two point four billion dollars of China sales last quarter.

So why does Stitch Loop matter for a product manager? Because it moves AI from helping you build a feature to proposing what to change next, based on your own metrics. The valuable skill becomes setting good goals and judging trade-offs, so think now about which of your product's numbers you would trust an agent to chase.

## Politics
Over to politics, and to Belgium first, where everyone seems to be waiting for someone else to pick up the phone.

According to Business AM, Prime Minister Bart De Wever has stopped direct talks with Vooruit and is working on a budget deal with the other coalition partners. Les Engagés, the MR and CD&V have accepted his budget framework as the starting point. Vooruit has not. Its position, as Business AM describes it, is that the richest should contribute more, and that planned savings on pensions, healthcare and indexation should be smaller. On Thursday evening, Vooruit's chairman Conner Rousseau posted that the party is simply asking for "a fair contribution from millionaires".

The political scientist Nicolas Bouteca of Ghent University told VRT radio that the situation has become more explosive since Frank Vandenbroucke's television interview earlier this week, in which the Vooruit minister attacked MR chairman Georges-Louis Bouchez and Foreign Minister Maxime Prévot. Bouteca said Vooruit, feeling cornered, could pull the plug, but that the perception of blame might also push the parties back to the table. His colleague Carl Devos told the programme Terzake that De Wever and Vooruit are each waiting for the other to call, and warned that without a call, Monday at the palace could go very badly.

A second Business AM piece reports speculation among some analysts that De Wever might replace Vooruit with Anders, the party formerly called Open VLD, which has proposed even deeper savings of sixteen billion euros. That's speculation, not a plan, and the arithmetic is tight. The coalition has eighty-one seats. Swapping Vooruit's thirteen for Anders' eight would leave seventy-six, the bare minimum, and the government would depend on the independent MP Jean-Marie Dedecker, who has reportedly called the idea unfeasible. Analysts quoted by Business AM see the exclusion of Vooruit as more likely a pressure tactic.

Markets are watching too. A new assessment of Belgium's creditworthiness by the rating agency Fitch is expected late today.

Also in the Chamber on Thursday, a debate after a VRT Pano investigation into neo-Nazi fight clubs. Justice Minister Annelies Verlinden called the phenomenon downright repugnant and said the services are tracking these networks as a priority. Interior Minister Bernard Quintin pointed again to his bill that would let the government ban radical organisations administratively, pending a decision by the courts.

Over to Europe, and to Berlin, where Chancellor Friedrich Merz hosted the so-called Coalition of the Willing on Thursday. According to NATO, more than thirty leaders attended, including President Zelensky and NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte, alongside the American envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner. Al Jazeera reports that Merz called the Shenzhen meeting a photo op and warned that a deal made without Europe would fail. France's President Emmanuel Macron called for more air defence and interceptors, and said the question of Ukraine joining NATO should be reopened. Kushner said Trump wants a deal with Europe by his side. No new commitments or figures were announced, and no date has been set for talks with Russia.

Which brings us to Shenzhen. On Thursday, Donald Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin held what Radio Free Europe calls the first three-way meeting of its kind, on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific summit. It lasted just over an hour. The result was a short four-point statement, published by China's state news agency Xinhua. It calls for reform of the United Nations, for more production of refined oil products and stable energy flows, and says bodies like the International Criminal Court must not abuse their jurisdiction. That matters because Putin is subject to an arrest warrant from that court. Finally, it repeats that a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought, and promises a dialogue on nuclear risk, with no date.

According to The Sunday Guardian, the statement doesn't mention Ukraine or Iran. Xi called the meeting positive and fruitful. Trump called it a highly consequential trip. President Zelensky called Trump's separate meeting with Putin a mistake, and said decisions on Ukraine must involve Ukraine. Analysts quoted by Radio Free Europe were divided on who gained most, with one calling Putin the biggest winner.

## Energy
Over to energy, and to Belgium first, where the cold has arrived and the price of power is feeling it.

Forecasters had flagged a cold spell over most of Europe from around today, and the wholesale market has moved ahead of it. According to the price tracker euenergy dot live, Belgium's day-ahead electricity price, the price set in the daily auction for the next day's power, stands at around two hundred and ten euros per megawatt-hour, up about eight per cent. That's close to Germany, at about two hundred and twenty-four, but far above France, at around a hundred and twenty-four, where nuclear output keeps prices down.

That gap matters for Belgium, which as we've reported has been importing record amounts of electricity. Households are already feeling it. Earlier this month, the consumer organisation Test-Aankoop found that fixed energy contracts had reached their highest level in almost four years, A typical Flemish household signing a new fixed contract for gas and electricity now would pay around fourteen hundred euros a year more than one that signed in March. Its advice: if you're on a fixed contract, stay put for now, because new offers are much more expensive.

The federal government's main relief so far is the so-called energy norm, which we covered at the start of the month. According to 21News, it took effect on the first of November, with lower grid charges for eligible companies and lower excise duties on electricity for everyone. The Energy Minister, Mathieu Bihet, has not said how much a typical household will save. Against prices like today's, that relief will be hard to notice.

And the nuclear clock keeps ticking. The Belgian state and Engie have until the twelfth of December to agree the main terms for the state to take over Engie's reactors. Both sides have said significant disagreements remain, mainly over the price.

Over to Europe, where gas is the root of the problem. According to Serbia Energy's weekly analysis, the main European benchmark, the Dutch TTF price, averaged about eighty-seven euros per megawatt-hour last week. A year ago it was around thirty, according to OilPrice. EU storage is estimated at seventy to seventy-two per cent full, against a five-year average close to eighty-nine. There is some relief: Europe is expected to receive about ten million tonnes of liquefied gas this month, more than in October. But OilPrice notes that German storage operators have warned supply may not be fully secured if this winter turns extremely cold.

And finally, the climate summit, COP31 in Antalya, which is due to end today. On Thursday, negotiators published a revised draft deal. According to Reuters, it removes every reference to fossil fuels, both versions we described yesterday, and also drops a target of a hundred and twenty billion dollars for adaptation finance, money to help countries cope with climate impacts. What remains is an acknowledgement that warming could overshoot one and a half degrees, a call for deep and rapid cuts, and support for Türkiye's goal of raising electricity's share of world energy use to thirty-five per cent by twenty thirty-five, from about twenty-three per cent now.

Reactions were sharp. Mohamed Adow of the think tank Power Shift Africa said the text had gone backwards, and warned of "a ghost COP". Australia's Chris Bowen, who is leading the negotiations, told UN News they would not leave Antalya without consensus. This is a draft, not the final deal, and these talks usually run well past their deadline, so expect news over the weekend.

## Term of the day
Today's term is opening auction. When a company lists on the stock market, nobody knows yet what the shares are really worth to the market. So instead of starting trade at the bell, the exchange first collects buy and sell orders, and then finds the single price at which the most shares can change hands. That's the opening auction. On Nasdaq, it's called the IPO Cross.

Think of a farmers' market on its first morning with a new kind of apple. Rather than haggling one by one, the stallholder collects everyone's offers and picks the price that clears the most crates.

It came up today because Anthropic's shares only started trading more than two hours after the bell. That wait was the auction gathering enough orders to set a first price, of a hundred and eleven dollars fifty.
