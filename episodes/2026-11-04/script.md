## AI
We start with AI, and with a change to the plumbing of AI agents rather than a new model.

A quick note first. This podcast is written with Claude, which is made by Anthropic, so I'll stick closely to what the reporting says.

Late on Monday, Anthropic put a feature called message threads into public beta in its developer platform. To see why it matters, you need to know how these systems normally work. A large language model doesn't remember anything between requests. Every time an app asks Claude for its next answer, it sends the entire conversation again, from the first message onwards. For a short chat, that's nothing. But for a coding agent that has been working for an hour, reading files and running tools, the conversation can be enormous.

With message threads, the app sends the full conversation once, and that starts a thread. After that, it only sends the new messages, plus a reference to the previous answer. Anthropic's servers rebuild the full context on their side. According to TokenPost and AlphaSignal, which report Anthropic's own figures, the median time to first token, meaning how long you wait before the answer starts to appear, fell by about thirty-five per cent for very long requests, of half a million to a million tokens. A token is roughly three quarters of a word. In long sessions of Claude Code, Anthropic's coding tool, the size of each request reportedly fell from about eight hundred kilobytes to under two.

There are clear limits, and they're worth spelling out. The bill doesn't change. The model still reads the full conversation on every turn, so you pay for the same tokens as before. Threads expire five minutes after the last request, after which the app has to send everything again. And developers are told to keep their own complete copy of the history, in case a thread disappears. TokenPost reports the thread data is encrypted and held in memory rather than written to disk. The feature is also available on the cloud platforms of Amazon, Microsoft and Google that resell Claude.

So who benefits? Mainly teams running long agents: coding assistants, agents that use a computer, workflows with lots of documents or images. For short chats, the reports say, the gain is small. It's also an architecture choice that other providers have made in their own ways. Some keep conversation state on their servers, others leave it entirely to the developer. Anthropic's version sits in between: the server holds the thread for a short time, but the app remains the owner of the full record. That means less data on the wire, without making the app depend on the provider's memory.

Over to OpenAI, where there's a model that hasn't been announced. TestingCatalog reports that a model called G P T six point two Astra appeared in the usage statistics of several users of Codex, OpenAI's coding agent, over the weekend. The share varied widely by account, from under two per cent of one user's week to more than a third of another user's day. One user shared a raw response showing that model name. The model picker still showed the current model, G P T six Astra, which suggests some requests are quietly being routed to the new one, perhaps as a test. To be clear, this is not a launch. OpenAI hasn't announced or commented on it, and the outputs people are sharing can't be tied to that model with certainty. Treat it as a rumour until OpenAI speaks.

One more tool for developers. According to the AI Agent Store's weekly news log, Google Cloud has released a developer plugin for its Antigravity coding environment. It bundles cloud skills, a connection to live Google Cloud documentation, and guardrails, such as checking access permissions and blocking destructive command-line instructions before they run. That last part is a pattern we see more and more: coding agents get safety rails built into the tool, rather than relying on the developer to spot a dangerous command.

Why does the threads story matter for a product manager? Because it's a reminder that the hard part of agent products is often state, not intelligence. Speed in long sessions now depends on how you manage conversation history, and the vendor features that help, like this one, come with expiry times and fallbacks that your product has to handle gracefully.

## Politics
Over to politics, and to Belgium first, where the King's consultations are well under way.

On Tuesday, King Philippe received the first two coalition party leaders. Both came because of Monday's offer by Prime Minister Bart De Wever to resign, after the budget talks broke down. The King has kept that decision on hold. First in was Yvan Verougstraete of Les Engagés. According to Anadolu Agency, he spent just over an hour at the palace and said he had brought "constructive ideas". The Brussels Times reports he said his party would keep negotiating until the very last moment. It also reports that he turned down an offer from Anders, the Flemish liberals who are in opposition, to explore an alternative majority.

Second was Sammy Mahdi of CD&V. Anadolu reports he criticised the MR and Vooruit for not showing enough urgency. He warned that Belgium could face "Greek conditions" if the deadlock continues, with markets reacting harshly and the welfare system at risk. His party colleague Vincent Van Peteghem, the budget minister, made a similar point on VRT. He said it's the second year in a row he is preparing the emergency regime of provisional twelfths, which lets the state keep spending month by month without a voted budget. He urged all partners to come down from what he called extreme positions.

The dividing line is still the same. Vooruit, CD&V and Les Engagés want the wealthiest to contribute more, while the N-VA and the MR oppose new taxes. VRT reports Les Engagés wants at least one billion euros from that group, and Vooruit one and a half billion. On the opposition side, the Socialist leader Paul Magnette said his party would not help out what he called a dying government.

The schedule, according to VRT: today it's the turn of Conner Rousseau of Vooruit and Georges-Louis Bouchez of the MR. Bouchez says he'll stop making public statements, to give the King's talks some room. Valerie Van Peel of the N-VA follows on Thursday afternoon, and De Wever returns to the palace on Friday at half past ten. VRT's political journalist Bart Verhulst says new elections would be the very last step.

## Energy
Over to energy, and to Belgium first, where today is a big day for Engie.

Today is the target date for the Belgian state and Engie to agree the heads of terms, the main conditions, for a state takeover of Engie's nuclear activities in Belgium. That date was set on the thirtieth of September, when both sides said they needed more time because of the deal's size and complexity. As of this morning, no announcement has been made. It's also the day Engie publishes its results for the third quarter, according to its financial calendar, so any update on the talks may come with those figures. The question remains whether a government whose resignation is on the King's desk can sign off on a deal of this size. We'll follow it tomorrow.

Staying with Belgium, Elia has published the results of three auctions in its capacity remuneration mechanism, known as the CRM. That's the system that pays power plants, batteries and flexible users to be available when the grid needs them, so the lights stay on in a tight winter. According to ESS News, which covers energy storage, batteries did very well. In the auction for the winter of twenty thirty to twenty thirty-one, four-hour batteries won more than sixty per cent of the contracted volume, well ahead of other technologies. In the auction for twenty twenty-eight to twenty twenty-nine, four-hour batteries took almost a third, behind gas-fired plants. In both auctions, about half a gigawatt of new battery capacity was selected, and supply was higher than demand. ESS News reports a weighted average price of about thirty-one thousand euros per megawatt per year for the twenty twenty-seven auction, falling to about eighteen thousand for the twenty thirty auction. The total cost of the twenty twenty-seven round was about two hundred million euros. The signal is clear: in Belgium's security of supply, batteries are no longer a side act.

Over to Europe's gas market. According to Trading Economics, the Dutch gas price, known as TTF, is trading around ninety-three euros per megawatt-hour this morning, after falling sharply on Monday on hopes of talks between the United States and Iran. That's still almost three times the level of a year ago. Storage remains the worry. European Union gas storage was around seventy-four and a half per cent on the second of November, which Trading Economics describes as the lowest on record for this time of year. Germany was at about sixty-one per cent, below its own seventy per cent target. Trading Economics' own forecast, which is a model and not a certainty, puts the price higher by the end of the quarter.

That pressure is feeding into politics. Euronews reports that the European Union's energy ministers ended their meeting in Luxembourg last week without agreement on protecting consumers. Thirteen countries, including Germany and Italy, asked the European Commission to withdraw its proposal to raise taxes on gas to favour cleaner energy, saying it would hurt low-income households. The Energy Commissioner Dan Jørgensen warned that nearly fifty million Europeans cannot adequately heat their homes. According to the Commission, member states have spent almost eighteen billion euros on short-term support this year, and it projects nearly thirty-nine billion by the end of the year. The Commission says it doesn't expect shortages of oil or gas this winter. Its main concern is an economic shock that makes basic energy unaffordable.

## Term of the day
Today's term is stateful, as opposed to stateless. A stateless system forgets everything between requests, so you have to tell it the whole story each time. A stateful system remembers where you left off. Think of a call centre. A stateless call centre is one where every time you call back, you explain your problem from the start. A stateful one has your case file open and asks what's new. It came up today because of Anthropic's message threads. AI models have always been stateless, so apps resend the whole conversation on every turn. Threads add a short-lived memory on the server side, which makes long sessions faster. But the memory expires after five minutes, so a well-built product must still be able to tell the whole story again when it has to.
