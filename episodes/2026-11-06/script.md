## Intro
Good morning Thomas, it's Friday the sixth of November. Today: Bart De Wever tells parliament the budget talks are a stinging failure, and goes back to the King this morning. OpenAI releases a security model it rates as critical risk, to a handful of vetted customers. And Engie raises its forecast while the Belgian nuclear talks drag on. Let's get into it.

## AI
We start with AI, and with a model that most people will never be allowed to use.

On Wednesday, OpenAI released GPT-6 Cyber, its fourth cybersecurity model this year. According to The Next Web, OpenAI rates it "Critical" for cybersecurity under its Preparedness Framework. That's the internal scale OpenAI uses to judge how much harm a model could do in the wrong hands, and critical is one step above the "High" rating of the previous version. Because of that, access is tightly limited. Only approved customers in what OpenAI calls Daybreak Red, a tier for exploit development and authorised security testing, can use it, through the developer interface and through Codex, OpenAI's coding agent.

What can it do? OpenAI points to two experiments. In the first, its researchers used the model on the Firefox browser and found eighty-one previously unknown high-severity flaws. According to Cyber Security News, Mozilla shipped fixes in a Firefox release at the end of October. The second experiment is more striking. OpenAI built an automatic pipeline that searched part of the Linux kernel, the core of the operating system that runs most of the world's servers. Cyber Security News reports that it examined about seven per cent of the kernel, reproduced one thousand seven hundred and twenty-four vulnerabilities, and built working attacks for nine hundred and forty-eight of them. An older model, in the same setup, managed one hundred and sixty-six. The run cost about one point two million dollars in computing credits, or roughly one thousand three hundred dollars per working attack. OpenAI says it sent three hundred and four critical findings to the Linux maintainers, with proposed patches.

OpenAI also stresses behaviour, not just power. In a test with decoy systems, the model stayed within its authorised targets in ninety-nine point nine three per cent of runs without any extra safety instructions, up from about ninety-four and a half per cent for the previous version. Not everyone is reassured. Chris Hughes of Noma Security, quoted by The Next Web, called that a real improvement, but warned that "even a small fraction means actions against systems nobody authorized." He also noted that fewer than a quarter of the flaws on OpenAI's own patching dashboard have been fixed so far. That's the uncomfortable part: finding bugs is getting cheap, while fixing them still depends on human maintainers.

Staying with OpenAI, a build-or-buy decision. According to Investing dot com, OpenAI has agreed to buy Pierre Computer Company, a small developer tools firm. Codex already uses Pierre's open-source tools to show file trees and code changes on screen, and Pierre also offers a code storage service. Terms weren't disclosed, the deal still needs regulatory approval, and Pierre's open-source projects will stay public. The idea, according to the report, is to turn Codex from a tool that writes code into a full place to store and review it. That puts OpenAI closer to territory long held by GitHub, which is owned by Microsoft, one of OpenAI's biggest partners. It's the second such deal this week, after Meta bought the coding startup Conductor, which we covered yesterday.

Next, a reality check on AI agents. On its earnings call on Wednesday, Uber said that in a recent week it handled about three hundred and twenty million rides and deliveries. According to Nation's Restaurant News, only about five hundred delivery orders, and just four rides, were booked by an outside AI agent. Uber's finance chief Balaji Krishnamurthy called it "very, very early and very limited traction." Chief executive Dara Khosrowshahi argued that Uber's own app is already quick, with a median reorder time of ninety seconds on Uber Eats. Uber still plans to work with outside agents, and is building its own.

And one more, briefly. A reminder that this podcast is written with Claude, made by Anthropic. On Thursday, Anthropic announced new security controls for business customers. According to a developer digest summarising the announcement, companies can now send prompts and planned tool actions to their existing security vendors before they run, so rules like data loss prevention still apply. A security centre that compares settings against Anthropic's recommendations is in beta, and Anthropic says it's guidance, not a compliance certificate.

So why does GPT-6 Cyber matter for a product manager? Because it shows a new release pattern. The most capable version of a model may arrive gated, with vetting and allow-lists, long before general access. And the Uber numbers are the other side of the coin: a capability existing doesn't mean customers will change how they buy. If agents are on your roadmap, measure real usage, not demos.

## Politics
Over to politics, and to Belgium first, where the Prime Minister has used a very strong word.

On Thursday afternoon, Bart De Wever addressed the Chamber. According to L'Avenir, he said that after a meeting of senior ministers last Sunday, it became clear that a complete multi-year budget could not be filed in time. So for the second year running, Belgium will start the year on provisional twelfths. That means the state may spend one twelfth of the previous budget each month, at least until the end of February. VRT quotes him calling it a terrible failure for the government, and that's why, he said, he offered his resignation. Asked whether that offer was serious, he replied that he always means everything he says. He also set a condition. VRT reports he only wants to stay on if the government's main task is a multi-year budget, because otherwise, in his view, it makes no sense.

De Wever defended his record. He cited an estimate by the International Monetary Fund that current measures improve the structural balance by almost two per cent of economic output by the end of the decade. And he warned about interest on the public debt: around sixteen billion euros this year, possibly more than fifty billion by twenty thirty-five.

The reactions split along familiar lines. Within the coalition, the MR's Georges-Louis Bouchez said it would be a disaster if the government fell. CD&V's Sammy Mahdi warned of what he called Greek conditions. In the opposition, Barbara Pas of Vlaams Belang called for elections. Paul Magnette of the PS said his party won't rescue a dying government. Groen spoke of a total embarrassment, and the PVDA repeated its call for a tax on the super-rich. Valerie Van Peel of the N-VA, the last party leader to see the King, said nothing on her way in or out.

What happens today? At half past ten, De Wever is due back at the palace to hear the King's conclusions. The King can refuse the resignation, keep it on hold, or accept it, which would most likely lead to elections. VRT's live blog expects him to refuse it and ask for new negotiations, but that's an expectation, not an announcement. L'Avenir says the options being discussed include restarting budget talks, renegotiating the coalition agreement, or a broad recovery plan. We'll know more by tonight.

Now to the European Union, and to a summit about AI. The European Council President, António Costa, has invited the twenty-seven leaders to an informal meeting in Dublin on the thirteenth of November. According to Eunews, it's the first leader-level discussion dedicated to artificial intelligence. The guests are Arthur Mensch, the chief executive of Mistral AI, and Geoffrey Hinton, the Nobel prize-winning AI researcher. Costa wrote that Europe "must define its position in this global race." No decisions are expected; it's a discussion.

Then to the wider world, and to Iran. Since Democrats won both chambers of Congress this week, the question in Washington is what President Trump does next. According to Axios, as reported by Gulf News, he's weighing new strikes, a deal, or continued economic pressure, and hasn't decided. Vice President JD Vance is urging an agreement. Qatar is trying to win support from both sides for a draft deal that could restart direct talks within days. Iran, for its part, demands an end to the American naval blockade, sanctions relief and the release of frozen funds before it reopens the Strait of Hormuz. Meanwhile, the fighting at sea goes on. CBS reports that more tankers were hit, and Iran's Revolutionary Guard says several vessels caught fire after hitting mines.

And the last Senate seats. In Mississippi, the Associated Press count on Thursday showed the Democrat Scott Colom ahead of Republican senator Cindy Hyde-Smith by about seventeen hundred votes, with ninety-six per cent counted. Provisional ballots, which Colom's campaign estimates at ten to fifteen thousand, still need to be checked, and the state must certify by the thirteenth of November. In Alaska, Democrat Mary Peltola leads senator Dan Sullivan by about six hundred votes. Because Alaska uses ranked-choice voting, the result likely won't be known before the eighteenth. President Trump says votes for a second candidate also named Dan Sullivan should go to the incumbent. Peltola's campaign denies a Justice Department accusation that it planted that candidate to confuse voters.

## Energy
Over to energy, and to Engie, which reported on Thursday while its Belgian nuclear talks remain unresolved.

According to Zonebourse, Engie's revenue over the first nine months of the year rose to fifty-four point three billion euros. Its operating profit excluding nuclear grew by about three per cent on a like-for-like basis, helped by UK Power Networks, the British grid operator it bought this year. Engie confirmed its targets for twenty twenty-six and now expects to land at the upper end of its range, with recurring net profit of up to five and a half billion euros. Analysts at Jefferies and Citi welcomed the numbers. There is a cost, though. Net financial debt rose by about eighteen billion euros since the end of last year, mostly because of that British purchase.

And Belgium? Engie's results repeat what we heard on Wednesday. Talks with the Belgian state about taking over its nuclear activities have been extended to the twelfth of December. Some progress has been made, but significant differences remain, mainly about valuation. With the federal government itself in limbo, it's not obvious who will sign on the Belgian side.

Staying in Belgium, energy companies also came up in parliament. According to L'Avenir, Paul Magnette of the PS argued during Thursday's debate that a windfall tax on energy companies, which he said raised about two billion euros under the previous government, should come back. Sarah Schlitz of Ecolo called for one before the end of the year, and estimated it could bring in one point three billion. The MR's Georges-Louis Bouchez replied that a one-off measure won't fix a structural deficit. These are opposition proposals, not government plans.

Over to European markets, where gas prices keep falling. According to Trading and Investment News, the Dutch benchmark, known as TTF, traded around eighty-five euros per megawatt-hour on Thursday morning, citing the bank ING. That's about sixteen per cent below last week's four-year peak, just above one hundred euros. Price reports vary by source and time of day, which is why that's lower than the figure we gave yesterday. Traders point to the Democratic wins in Congress and the President's remark that there are no immediate plans to strike Iran. But supply is still tight. QatarEnergy has extended its force majeure, a legal notice that it can't deliver, on liquefied gas until at least the end of December. And European storage was only about seventy-four per cent full on Tuesday, well below the five-year average. ING warns this leaves the market vulnerable as winter deepens.

And finally, a startup. Green Voltis, a Stockholm company founded in twenty twenty-four, has raised thirteen and a half million euros in a so-called Series A round, according to the startup site Pomegra. It trades electricity from large batteries and solar and wind parks on behalf of their owners, using its own price-forecasting AI. It says it has more than one point two gigawatt-hours of storage under contract, across ten European countries. The only investor is a fund linked to CATL, the Chinese battery giant. Part of the money goes to a plan to supply power to data centres in Scandinavia, though no customers have been named. It's a small deal, but a telling one: the software that decides when a battery charges and discharges is becoming a business in its own right, and big hardware makers want a piece of it.

## Term of the day
Today's term is privilege escalation. It came up in OpenAI's Linux experiment, where all nine hundred and forty-eight working attacks were of this kind. In plain words, it means getting more power on a computer than you're supposed to have. Think of a hotel. Your key card opens your room and the gym. Privilege escalation is finding a flaw that turns that card into the manager's master key, so suddenly every door opens. On a computer, an attacker might start as an ordinary user, or through a small hacked app, and then use a bug to become the administrator, who can read everything and change anything. That's why these bugs are taken so seriously, and why it matters that the patches reach the maintainers quickly.
