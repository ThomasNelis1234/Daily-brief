## Intro
Good morning Thomas, it's Friday the thirteenth of November, and this is your Daily Brief. Today: Jensen Huang has resigned as chief executive of Nvidia, the company at the centre of the AI boom. Bart De Wever restarts his budget talks this afternoon, with the King waiting on Monday. And new energy contracts in Flanders are the most expensive since early twenty twenty-three. Let's begin.

## AI
We start with AI, and with the biggest leadership change the industry has seen in years.

Jensen Huang, who co-founded Nvidia and ran it for more than three decades, has resigned. In a filing with the American markets regulator dated Thursday, Nvidia says Huang has stepped down as president, chief executive and board member, with immediate effect. Colette Kress, the chief financial officer, becomes interim chief executive while keeping her finance role. And the board has appointed its first ever chair, Harvey Jones, a former head of the chip design software company Synopsys. The filing gives no reason for the departure. It says the board will look at internal and external candidates for a permanent successor.

The background, according to Al Jazeera, drawing on Reuters, comes from a week of reporting by American newspapers. The New York Times and the Wall Street Journal reported on personal relationships of Huang's, including one with a former executive of a Singapore company that American officials suspect of links to Chinese intelligence. China denies that. In a memo to staff on Sunday, Huang admitted he had been unfaithful to his wife. The board had opened an independent investigation into whether he was the target of a foreign intelligence effort, and the Wall Street Journal then reported that it was preparing to remove him. Al Jazeera notes that Nvidia lost more than six hundred billion dollars in market value over the week. President Trump said the FBI is looking into the matter. Three Republican committee chairs in the House of Representatives had also asked Huang to attend a closed-door briefing on the eighteenth of November. Huang remains a large shareholder, with about three point six per cent of the company, according to Al Jazeera. None of these allegations has been tested in any formal process.

Why does this matter beyond the boardroom? Nvidia makes most of the chips on which AI models are trained and run, so almost every AI product depends on it somewhere. Its next quarterly results come on Tuesday the seventeenth, and investors will be watching for any sign that customers or plans are affected.

Now to a story that sits right on the desk of product managers. United Airlines says it will stop AI agents from booking its flights. According to Engadget, the restriction covers United's own website as well as travel agencies and flight comparison sites. An AI assistant may still show you United's flights, but a person has to complete the purchase. United gives several reasons. Handing an agent your payment details or login could lead to unintended purchases, changes to your account, or your air miles being spent. Some steps are required by aviation rules to be done by a human, such as confirming that your checked bag contains no explosives. And there's a commercial reason too. Chief executive Scott Kirby said this week, quote, "I don't want to create another middleman." Not every airline agrees. Engadget points out that Southwest offers a ChatGPT plugin for booking. It also notes how small agent shopping still is. Uber said AI agents placed about five hundred food orders and just four rides in the week of Halloween, out of three hundred and twenty million trips and deliveries.

One more launch, for the builders. Google has released the Gemini Decisions API, running on a new model called Gemini Decision 1. According to Google's own blog, it doesn't write text at all. Instead, you send it one input, such as a document, an image or a video, plus up to a thousand closed questions: yes or no, multiple choice, or a score on a scale. It returns an answer to each with a confidence score between zero and one, in a fraction of a second. Google prices it at ten cents per million input tokens, with nothing charged for output. The intended uses are things like routing a request to the right model, checking content against a policy, or grading answers. Those are Google's claims, and independent tests will follow.

So why does United's decision matter for a product manager? Yesterday we heard Adobe opening its tools to outside agents. United is doing the opposite. Both are product decisions about who owns the customer relationship. If agents start acting for your users, you'll need a clear policy: which actions an agent may take, which must stay with a human, and how you'll prove who did what.

## Politics
Over to politics, and to Belgium first, where the clock is running towards Monday.

The Prime Minister, Bart De Wever, has scheduled a fresh round of talks for this afternoon. According to VRT News, he will see his vice-prime ministers one by one from two o'clock, and may bring the full core cabinet together in the evening. That would be the first meeting of the core cabinet since the King refused De Wever's resignation last week. The weekend has reportedly been kept free for more talks. On Monday, De Wever goes back to the palace to report to King Philippe. As VRT sketched earlier this week, that leaves three broad outcomes: an agreement, another extension of the deadline, or the fall of the government. The gap to close is still large. The MR wants new taxes capped at about one point one billion euros, out of a total effort of around ten billion.

In the Chamber on Thursday, De Wever said he hoped to bring the King a positive message. He would not comment on the content, to give the talks every chance, and said that agreeing a budget was very difficult, as it is elsewhere in Europe. De Standaard reported on Thursday that hopes of a breakthrough were low, because the question of whether party presidents should join the table is paralysing the talks.

The parties' tone varies. Georges-Louis Bouchez, president of the MR, told VRT he expects progress in the coming days, but couldn't say whether there would be a deal or an interim result by Monday. He said all the building blocks for an agreement are there. What matters most, he said, is that the country gets a budget, more than how it gets there. Axel Ronse of the N-VA said failing to reach a deal would be, in his words, criminal. Les Engagés have put a specific demand on the table: they want a transitional scheme for unemployed people who care for a relative extended until twenty twenty-nine. Their member of parliament Anne Pirson warned that a solution is needed if the party is to stay on board. Minister David Clarinval of the MR confirmed the topic is being discussed, but said unemployment cannot be the answer for carers. From the opposition, Barbara Pas of Vlaams Belang said new elections are the only alternative.

That brings us to the European Union, and to Dublin. On Thursday, around fifty leaders met there for the European Political Community, a broad forum that goes well beyond the twenty-seven member states. Today, the leaders of the Union meet informally on their own. According to The Brussels Times, António Costa, the President of the European Council, planned this as the first discussion dedicated to artificial intelligence at leaders' level, on competitiveness and what he calls AI sovereignty, meaning less dependence on foreign technology. The expected guests include Arthur Mensch, head of the French AI company Mistral, and the Nobel Prize-winning scientist Geoffrey Hinton. But EUobserver reports that the war in Ukraine has pushed AI down the agenda. Leaders will hold a first debate on a possible EU envoy for the peace process, discussing its role and mandate, with names not expected before the December summit. As we heard yesterday, Bart De Wever is skipping Dublin to focus on the budget.

Which brings us to Ukraine. On Thursday, the American envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Vladimir Putin in Moscow, then flew to Dublin to brief President Zelenskyy. According to the Indian news agency ANI, as published by The Tribune, the Kremlin said Putin highlighted progress on humanitarian issues, such as exchanges of detainees. Zelenskyy said Ukraine was, quote, "counting on the largest prisoner exchange since the start of this war." No numbers or date have been given. He also said Europe should have an equal seat at the negotiating table, and Witkoff said European countries are partners who should be involved. Moscow sounds more sceptical. Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov said the three-way talks would continue, but questioned a European role, saying Europeans must decide whether they want peace or continued war. The big differences remain territory and security guarantees. Putin repeated that foreign troops in Ukraine after a peace deal would be unacceptable to Russia. Witkoff said a new round of talks has been set, without saying when.

## Energy
Over to energy, and to Belgium first, where the cold, still weather of this week is reaching household bills.

According to the Flemish utilities regulator, the VNR, as reported by VRT News, an average Flemish household signing a new variable contract in November can expect an annual electricity bill of about one thousand five hundred and forty-six euros. That's around a hundred euros more than in October. The estimated annual gas bill for a new contract is about two thousand three hundred and fifty-six euros, more than three hundred euros up on October. Both are the highest since January twenty twenty-three. These figures are estimates for new contracts. People on fixed-price contracts are not affected for now.

The reasons are the ones we've followed all week. Gas is expensive, and in hours with little wind and sun, gas power stations set the price of electricity. VRT reported on Wednesday that on Monday evening more than half of Belgium's power came from gas plants, at a moment when the Tihange 3 reactor had briefly shut down after a problem during a test. Engie had it back at full power by Wednesday.

Over to the European gas market. According to Eurasia Business News, which draws on Bloomberg and industry data, the Dutch benchmark gas price, known as TTF, rose above eighty-seven euros per megawatt-hour on Wednesday, after a dip to about eighty-two. Gas Infrastructure Europe figures show the Union's storage was about seventy-two per cent full on Tuesday, well below the usual level for this time of year. Withdrawals rose almost sixfold in a week as the cold set in. Bloomberg, as published by Energy Connects, reports that Germany's storage was only fifty-nine per cent full. And supply from the Persian Gulf remains disrupted, despite President Trump's signals that further strikes on Iran may not be needed. QatarEnergy has extended its force majeure on deliveries until at least the end of December. Force majeure is a legal clause that lets a supplier skip deliveries because of events beyond its control. Most weather models still point to a mild winter. But the European gas network operators' association has a stress scenario, not a forecast, in which a cold winter and tight supply leave storage barely ten per cent full by spring.

Finally, the climate summit, COP31, in Antalya in Türkiye, where world leaders finished their speeches on Thursday. Reuters reports that talks on a plan to move away from fossil fuels have stalled. Brazil, which hosted last year's summit, has published a voluntary roadmap under which countries would assess their energy systems by twenty twenty-eight and start cutting fossil fuel supply from twenty thirty-one. The UN Secretary-General, António Guterres, urged clear timelines, saying the transition should no longer be treated as taboo. The United States is absent for the second year running. On a separate track, Climate Home News reports that the EU, small island states and African countries are pushing for a roadmap on the oceans, and that no country spoke against it. The negotiators now have to turn the speeches into agreed text.

## Term of the day
Today's term is a calibrated confidence score. When an AI system gives an answer with a number between zero and one, that number is calibrated if it can be trusted at face value. If the system says ninety per cent sure on a thousand answers, about nine hundred of them should be right. Think of a weather forecaster. If she says seventy per cent chance of rain on ten days, it should rain on about seven of them. It came up today because Google's new Decisions API returns a confidence score with every answer, and Google claims its model has the lowest calibration error in its tests. For a product team, a well-calibrated score is useful: you can let the system act alone when it's confident and send the uncertain cases to a person.
