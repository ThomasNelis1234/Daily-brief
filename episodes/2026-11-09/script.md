## Intro

## AI
We start with AI, and with an idea that could change what an app even is.

On Friday, Meta added a feature called Spaces to Muse, its AI assistant app. The idea is simple. You describe what you want, and Muse builds a small, interactive tool for you, inside the app. According to Digital Trends, Meta's examples include a trip planner, a wedding planner, a wish list for gifts, a tracker that alerts you to concert dates, and a dashboard for school. You don't write any code. If you want a change, you just ask: add a feature, change the look, alter how it behaves. Digital Trends sums it up as an app within an app.

Two details make this more than a toy. First, you can decide where a Space lives. You can pin it to the sidebar or the main tabs, hide it, or schedule it, so that a weekly work planner shows up on weekdays and gives way to weekend plans on Saturday. Second, Spaces can connect to other apps you use, and then update themselves in the background, for example with price changes or progress towards a savings goal. Meta hasn't published which apps it can connect to.

According to FourWeek MBA, Meta says it's also rolling out new, specialised models for Muse, which it claims are much less likely to forget important information during a task. That's Meta's own claim, not an independent test. Sharing a Space with other people isn't possible yet; Meta says it is exploring it. And the same site points to a weakness: earlier data suggested that many people who try Muse stop using it within two weeks. Building your own tools could be Meta's answer to that, by giving people a reason to come back.

Meta isn't first here. Google, Replit and others already let people generate small apps from a prompt. But Meta has billions of users, and many of them, as one Meta executive put it, had never used AI before Muse.

Now to the money behind all this. On Friday, Bloomberg reported that OpenAI has raised another eleven billion dollars, at a valuation of one point two five trillion dollars. TechCrunch, following that report, notes that the round hasn't closed and could reach about twenty billion. But that's well below what Bloomberg reported in September: OpenAI was then seeking thirty billion at a valuation of one point four trillion. Hours earlier, Bloomberg also reported that potential buyers of shares in Anthropic's planned stock market listing are pricing it well below what Anthropic hoped. Some think it could be worth as little as one point one trillion dollars, against expectations of around two trillion a month ago, according to CNBC. TechCrunch links the cooler mood partly to last week's Democratic wins in the United States, and to strong voter hostility towards data centres. These are reports based on unnamed sources, not final figures. For transparency, Anthropic makes Claude, the model that writes this script.

And one more from OpenAI, about whether AI can be trusted in a laboratory. On Friday, OpenAI and the enzyme design company Ligo released a test called BioBasicsBench. It contains one hundred routine lab tasks, based on raw material: photos of gels and petri dishes, microscope images, sequencing data and even short videos from the lab bench. Nineteen of the questions check whether the model recognises that the evidence simply isn't enough to draw a conclusion. According to a write-up by Winzheng, OpenAI's best model, GPT-6 Astra, scored about sixty-seven per cent. An older model, GPT-five point two, scored around thirty-one. But the best model still made basic mistakes, such as choosing the wrong colony in a cloning task and overcounting another plate by about twelve per cent. OpenAI itself warns that a higher score does not yet mean the model can make reliable judgements in the lab. And the results haven't been independently reproduced.

So why does the Meta story matter for a product manager? Because it shifts the unit of software from the app you build for millions to the tool each user builds for themselves. If your product is a simple tracker or planner, your competitor may now be a sentence typed into an assistant. The questions to ask are what your product does that a generated tool can't, such as trusted data, shared workflows or real integrations, and whether you should be the place where those generated tools connect.

## Politics
Over to politics, and to Belgium first, where the government lives on, for now.

On Friday morning, Bart De Wever went back to the palace. The King refused his resignation. According to The Brussels Times, the palace said the King has asked him to resume negotiations for a budget. De Wever now has ten days. He's due to report back to the King on Monday the sixteenth of November. Euronews reports that the talks with the leaders of the five coalition parties start again tomorrow, Tuesday. The palace also said that those five leaders made a formal commitment to make concessions in order to reach a deal. That's an unusual public promise, and it raises the stakes for each of them.

The gap is still large. De Wever wants about ten billion euros by twenty twenty-nine to bring the deficit down. The dividing lines haven't changed. According to The Brussels Times, Vooruit, CD&V and Les Engagés want higher taxes on what they call the strongest shoulders. The MR and the N-VA strongly oppose that. Euronews adds that a rise in value added tax is on the table, which the MR rejects. The background is serious. The Brussels Times puts Belgium's deficit at five point two per cent of economic output, the highest in the eurozone, with debt expected above one hundred and ten per cent this year. Even with a deal, it's too late to pass a full budget into law before January, so Belgium will start the year on provisional twelfths either way. What's at stake now is whether the coalition survives, and on what plan.

Now to the European Union, and to a case about how social media apps choose what you see. On Friday, the European Commission said, in preliminary findings, that TikTok's For You feed breaks the Digital Services Act, the EU law that governs large online platforms. According to the Associated Press, the Commission says TikTok failed to assess how its recommendations can pull users into rabbit holes, for example videos about weight loss that could harm mental health. It wants TikTok to make it easier to opt out of certain content, to give more weight to what users explicitly say they want, and to stop using viewing time as a key signal for recommendations. The Commission's Executive Vice-President Henna Virkkunen said the algorithm serves more of the same. TikTok called the findings fundamentally inaccurate and says it has a strong record on user safety. This isn't a final decision. TikTok can respond, and a fine could reach six per cent of its global annual revenue.

Then to the United States. On Friday, President Trump announced that Stephen Miller will become White House chief of staff, replacing Susie Wiles. According to PBS NewsHour, Miller is currently deputy chief of staff and is best known as the architect of the administration's hard-line immigration policy. Wiles, the first woman in the job, said serving the President had been the honour of a lifetime, and Trump said she would keep working for his political organisation. The change comes days after Republicans lost control of both chambers of Congress.

Staying with Washington, and with Iran. According to ABC News in Australia, Trump said on Friday that American forces had carried out new, limited strikes on an Iranian air defence system, which he called minor. Fox News reports he also said there's no return to a major war. Meanwhile, Qatar's mediation continues. The Associated Press reports that Iran is reviewing Qatari proposals on its nuclear programme, but officials say Tehran won't negotiate on that until Washington meets several conditions, including sanctions relief and an end to the naval blockade. At sea, Iran's military said on Sunday that two tankers caught fire after hitting mines in the Strait of Hormuz, and the American military said it disabled a ship that tried to break the blockade.

Finally, Ukraine. Tomorrow and Wednesday, American-mediated talks between Ukraine and Russia take place in Abu Dhabi, the first three-way round since February. According to the Kyiv Independent, the Kremlin has upgraded its team. Presidential aide Vladimir Medinsky leads it, and for the first time it includes the deputy foreign minister Sergei Ryabkov, alongside the head of military intelligence. Bloomberg reads that as a sign Moscow may want to discuss political and military issues in more depth. Ukraine's delegation is led by Rustem Umerov. The fighting goes on. Russian missiles hit Kyiv on Saturday, damaging a major bridge and injuring at least six people, and the death toll from last week's strike on an aid centre in the Zaporizhzhia region has risen to fifty-five.

## Energy
Over to energy, and to Belgium first, where the government crisis now overlaps with the biggest energy file on the table.

Remember the two deadlines. The King expects a budget deal by the sixteenth of November. And the Belgian state and Engie have given themselves until the twelfth of December to agree the main terms of a state takeover of Engie's nuclear activities in Belgium. According to Energy Minister Mathieu Bihet's office, that's the date both sides agreed last week. The deal would cover all seven reactors, the staff, and the costs of dismantling them, and World Nuclear News reports that the main sticking point is still the price. Only two of those reactors still run, Doel 4 and Tihange 3, and an earlier agreement already extended them until twenty thirty-five, with the state and Engie as equal owners. The new deal would go much further, putting the whole nuclear business in public hands. If the coalition falls apart, it's unclear who could sign such a deal. If it holds, nuclear could become one more bargaining chip in the budget talks.

For households, the backdrop is energy bills. A survey by the European Investment Bank, published last Thursday and reported by The Brussels Times, found that forty-nine per cent of Belgians say their purchasing power fell over the past year because of energy prices. About two in five turned their heating down, more than the EU average. And fifteen per cent went into debt, sought help, or paid an energy bill late.

Over to European markets, where there's some relief. According to the Al Attiyah Foundation's weekly review, the Dutch gas benchmark, known as TTF, fell almost eighteen per cent last week. Analysts point to softer American language on Iran and expectations of more supply from Qatar. Trading Economics showed the price around eighty-six euros per megawatt-hour on Monday. That's still almost three times higher than a year ago. And the same source puts European gas storage at about seventy-four per cent full, against a five-year average of around ninety. So the market is calmer, but not comfortable.

And that matters because of the weather. Euronews warns that Europe may be heading into its first dark, windless spell of the winter, when solar and wind output drop together and the grid leans on gas. Solar's share of EU electricity fell from about a quarter in June to just over thirteen per cent in October. Euronews also reports that solar has saved the EU around forty-three billion euros in gas imports since the war with Iran began in February.

There's a telling side effect. Bloomberg, via Energy Connects, reports that negative power prices, the hours when there's so much electricity that producers pay to get rid of it, almost disappeared in October. Germany had just two such hours, against forty-eight a year earlier, even though wind output was strong. The reasons are expensive gas, which lifts all prices, and more batteries and electrolysers soaking up cheap power. The firm Energy Aspects expects that trend to continue through the winter. For battery owners, that's a mixed picture: fewer chances to charge for free, but bigger price swings to earn from.

## Term of the day
Today's term is dunkelflaute. It's a German word that roughly means a dark, windless lull. It describes a period, often several days in winter, when it's cloudy and there's hardly any wind, so solar panels and wind turbines both produce very little at the same time.

Think of a household that runs on two sources of income, and both employers stop paying in the same week. Savings, or in this case batteries, gas plants and imports, have to cover the gap, and that's when it gets expensive.

It came up today because Euronews warns that Europe may face its first dunkelflaute of the season, with gas storage below normal. For Belgium, with fewer nuclear reactors running, these weeks are exactly what Elia's capacity auctions are designed for.
