## Intro
Good morning Thomas, it's Friday the thirtieth of October. Here's your Daily Brief. Today: Google unveils Gemini 4 Carbon, a new top-ranked model that only cyber defenders can use for now. Belgium's core cabinet switches to daily budget talks, weekends included. And Israel's final count leaves neither Netanyahu nor his rivals with a majority.

## AI
We start with AI, and with a model you can't use yet.

On Wednesday, Google announced Gemini 4 Carbon, its new frontier model. It comes just two weeks after Gemini 4 Argon, which itself only reached the wider public on the thirteenth of October. According to Engadget, Google pitches Carbon at two things: long, complicated software engineering, and cybersecurity, meaning finding weak spots in code, ranking how serious they are, and fixing their root cause. Google says Carbon is twice as likely as Argon to write secure code, and better at resisting prompt injection. That's when hidden instructions in a web page or a document try to hijack the model.

On the numbers, VentureBeat reports that the independent benchmarking firm Artificial Analysis puts Carbon first on its general intelligence index, with fifty-nine points. Anthropic's Claude Opus five point five has fifty-eight, and OpenAI's GPT six Astra fifty-seven. So it's a narrow lead, not a runaway. And the independent picture is mixed. On Artificial Analysis's coding agent index, Carbon scores fifty-nine, behind Claude Code running Opus at sixty-two. Carbon also scored highest on a test of factual knowledge, but VentureBeat notes its rate of made-up answers rose from four per cent with Argon to eleven per cent.

The more interesting part is how Google is releasing it. According to Engadget, Carbon first goes only to selected cybersecurity defence companies, then to trusted partners in Google's Fairwind programme, and only later to everyone, with no date given. Google says it wants feedback first and to refine its safeguards. Carbon is also taking part in the United States government's voluntary testing before release. The reasoning is simple. A model that is very good at finding security holes is useful to defenders, but just as useful to attackers. So Google lets the defenders patch first. Argon followed the same path, and reached the public about two weeks after launch.

Prices are already set. VentureBeat reports an introductory rate of two dollars per million input tokens and ten dollars per million output tokens, rising later to four and twenty. A token is a small chunk of text, roughly three quarters of a word. Google also wrote that internally, its teams have seen Carbon run for days on a single task. That's Google's claim, and one to test once access opens.

Next, from the same day, a launch about choosing models rather than building them. At its Universe conference in San Francisco, GitHub made Copilot Fusion generally available. Until now, developers using Copilot picked a model themselves for each task. Fusion makes that choice for them. According to The New Stack, it looks at the task, then either gives it to one model, or uses a cascade, where a cheaper model tries first and a quality check escalates to a stronger one if needed, or has one model critique another's work. Developers choose an effort level: standard, high or max. Company administrators can limit which models are allowed.

GitHub claims the max setting comes within two points of Claude Code with Opus on the Artificial Analysis coding index, at about half the cost per task and with forty per cent less time. Those are GitHub's own figures. An AT and T engineer quoted by The New Stack explained the problem it solves: developers would pick a powerful model for a hard task, and then forget to switch back.

GitHub also published its yearly Octoverse report. The Next Web reports that AI agents opened more than forty per cent of pull requests on GitHub in September, up from seventeen per cent in March. A pull request is a proposed change to a codebase, waiting for review. GitHub says that is a minimum, because it can't detect every agent. In September alone, that meant around one hundred and ten million pull requests. That's a big shift in who, or what, writes the first draft of software.

So why does this matter for a product manager? Two lessons. Google's staged rollout shows that for risky capabilities, who gets access first is a product decision, not just a safety footnote. And GitHub's Fusion shows where AI products are heading: users shouldn't have to pick a model. If your product exposes a model menu, ask whether routing could hide that choice and cut your costs.

## Politics
Over to politics, and to Belgium first.

The federal budget talks have moved into marathon mode. According to Belga, as reported by La DH, the core cabinet met on Wednesday from seven in the evening until just before midnight, without a deal. From now on, the Prime Minister and the main ministers will meet every day. La Libre reports the sessions run from two in the afternoon until midnight, weekends included, with the aim of a deal early next week.

How close are they? La Libre writes that the five coalition parties have already approved many of the savings and revenue measures, and that about one and a half billion euros remain to be found. The overall goal is an effort of around ten billion euros by twenty twenty-nine. If a deal comes in time, Prime Minister Bart De Wever could give his policy speech to the Chamber next Tuesday, the third of November.

The positions are familiar, but sharper. De Wever wants a budget that respects the European spending rules and calms the financial markets, and he has said he won't accept a fallback to so-called provisional twelfths. That's the emergency regime where the state may spend, each month, one twelfth of the previous year's budget. Vooruit leader Conner Rousseau disagrees. He says provisional twelfths are, in his words, not the end of the world, and he wants a fairer package in which the wealthiest contribute more. La Libre reports that Vooruit objects to a proposal to limit burn-out benefits to two years. The MR is wary of tax increases. Les Engagés leader Maxime Prévot told VRT NWS the effort in health care is still disproportionate. And CD&V's Sammy Mahdi says the country doesn't have the luxury of waiting. VRT's political journalists note that without a deal early next week, the budget can't be voted on before the end of the year. La Libre even sketches a scenario in which, after a long stalemate, De Wever could offer his resignation to the King. That's speculation, not a stated plan.

Now to Europe, and to the war in Ukraine, where Europe's role is again in question. According to the Kyiv Post, the Kremlin's foreign policy aide Yuri Ushakov told the Russian agency Interfax on Thursday that the next round of talks between Russia, Ukraine and the United States could take place in Abu Dhabi, sometime after the fourth of November. No date is confirmed. Ushakov said the timing depends on the situation on the battlefield. He also said there was absolutely no discussion of inviting European representatives. President Zelenskyy said on Wednesday the talks could happen on the tenth of November. Ushakov blamed Ukraine for derailing the previous round in September. Kyiv has blamed Moscow.

Over to the world, and to Israel, where the count is now complete. Al Jazeera reports that with more than ninety-nine per cent of ballots counted, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc has fifty-three seats, the opposition bloc led by Gadi Eisenkot forty-eight, and the Arab-led parties nineteen. A majority needs sixty-one. The results become official once the Central Elections Committee certifies them, by next Wednesday.

The mood has turned bitter. Al Jazeera reports that Netanyahu's Likud has promoted claims, without evidence, that Arab-led parties stole the election, possibly with foreign help. The head of the elections committee said he received a letter alleging foreign interference, could not verify it, and asked the Shin Bet security service to investigate. Eisenkot accuses Netanyahu of trying to delegitimise the result. Eisenkot has met Naftali Bennett and Yair Golan to explore a government, and Golan met Ra'am leader Mansour Abbas. Analysts quoted by Al Jazeera say the opposition can't govern without Arab-led parties, but that such a coalition remains unlikely.

And finally, the United States, where the midterm elections are next Tuesday. Al Jazeera reports that Democrats lead the national polling average by about eight points, fifty-one to forty-three. Democrats need a net gain of three seats to take the House of Representatives, and four to take the Senate, which is much harder. Close Senate races include Texas, Michigan, North Carolina, Ohio and Georgia. Polls are a snapshot, not a result. Donald Trump is rallying in South Carolina, Florida and Kansas this week, while Barack Obama and Kamala Harris campaign in Georgia.

## Energy
Over to energy, and to Belgium first, where nuclear power has a new club and a new critic.

On Thursday, Belgium became a full member of the European Nuclear Alliance, after years as an observer. According to Business AM, Energy Minister Mathieu Bihet confirmed the move, and Belgium will chair the alliance's next meeting. The group, led by France since twenty twenty-three, now counts fifteen of the twenty-seven member states. It lobbies the European Commission to treat nuclear on equal terms with renewables in the climate rules after twenty thirty. Bihet said nuclear is again a cornerstone of Belgium's energy strategy. It's a clear break with the phase-out law of about two decades ago.

Meanwhile, the deadline with Engie is next Wednesday, the fourth of November. Yesterday we heard one economist argue that nationalising the plants could make sense. Today, a sharper warning. L'Avenir spoke to Dominique Woitrin, a nuclear engineer who helped design Doel and Tihange and later headed CREG, the federal energy regulator. He calls nationalisation a potential financial disaster: the state would take on all the risks, while the profits went to Paris. He argues Engie should pay an extra two and a half to three billion euros for dismantling the plants. In his view, restarting the older reactors is unrealistic, and the sensible option is to extend Doel four and Tihange three beyond twenty thirty-five, if the safety regulator agrees. Engie didn't comment. These are one expert's views, not the government's.

Over to Europe, where yesterday's meeting of energy ministers in Luxembourg produced more argument than answers. As expected, there were no decisions on prices. European Energy Commissioner Dan Jørgensen told reporters, according to Eunews, that more than fifty million Europeans can't heat their homes adequately, and that this winter will be difficult. He urged governments to use targeted, temporary support for the most vulnerable.

The real fight was over a Commission proposal to cut electricity bills over time. Euronews reports that thirteen countries, including Germany, Italy and Poland, want to scrap a provision that would tax electricity more lightly than gas, to push people towards heat pumps and electric cars. Spain, Denmark and Luxembourg back the idea but want flexibility. France wants a middle path. A second dispute is over grid charges, the part of your bill that pays for the network. The Commission wants to set common rules for how they're calculated. Italy, France, Germany and others say that would undermine national regulators. Jørgensen said the plan makes a lot of sense. The Irish presidency hopes for a compromise in December. Sweden's energy minister Ebba Busch called this the second energy crisis in four years.

The price backdrop eased only slightly. According to Trading Economics, the Dutch gas benchmark fell to about ninety-seven euros per megawatt-hour on Thursday, after touching one hundred the day before. European storage was about seventy-five per cent full, against a five-year average of around eighty-six. In Germany, it's below sixty-two per cent. Germany's deputy energy minister told his colleagues in Luxembourg that, unusually, German storage saw no withdrawals at all in October, and even a slight injection, thanks to steady imports and fully working liquefied gas terminals. Trading Economics also points to forecasts of colder weather in north-western Europe, which could mean more competition for gas cargoes with Asia. For Belgian households, the usual caveat applies: bills follow wholesale prices with a delay, so the full effect is expected next year.

## Term of the day
Today's term is model routing. Instead of sending every request to the same AI model, a router looks at each task first and decides which model should handle it. Easy jobs go to a small, cheap, fast model. Hard ones go to a big, expensive one. Think of a hospital triage desk: a sprained ankle doesn't need the head surgeon, but a heart problem does. A common variant is the cascade, where the cheap model tries first, and a check sends the answer up to a stronger model only if it isn't good enough. It came up today because GitHub's Copilot Fusion does exactly this for coding agents, and claims it halves the cost per task. For product teams, routing is quickly becoming a standard way to control AI costs without users noticing.
